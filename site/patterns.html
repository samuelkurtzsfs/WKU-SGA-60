The patterns

The same arguments come back. Campus lighting, the price of a textbook, who is in the room, whether anybody voted: the subjects that recur across sixty years of student government at Western, set out with the years they recur in.

This page holds 41 recurring patterns and 428 dated instances, every one of them drawn from a sourced entry on this site and checked against the record. Each pattern gives its span, a spread of instances across different decades, and what changed between the first and the last. Where the record is thin, that is said rather than smoothed over. The story reads the same archive in order, and what SGA put on reads it by what the organization staged.

What it does, generation after generation

Fifteen subjects the organization has come back to every four or five years since 1966, wearing whatever clothes the decade supplied. What changes is not the subject but SGA’s position in relation to it.

The strongest single finding in this material is a change of role rather than of subject. For its first thirty years SGA asked the university to do things: put a telephone there, open the library later, run a shuttle, assign staff to administer scholarships. For its last thirty it increasingly does them itself, out of its own budget: $600 a year to keep the library open until 2 a.m., a $15,000 retroactive bill for a year of the safe-ride van, $23,500 in scholarships, 450 Uber vouchers, a shelf of borrowable graphing calculators, $10,000 split among 33 clubs.

Petitioner to vendor. The organization converts from an applicant for university action into a small vendor of student services, and calls that its central programme. The arc runs from Resolution 79-10, which asked WKU to assign somebody to administer scholarships, to a $100,000 budget in 2024 that awarded them itself.

Campus safety, lighting and getting home at night1983–2026 · 15 instances

Lighting, crosswalks, emergency telephones, escorts and rides home. Nothing on lighting or night safety appears in this archive before 1983, which is a fact about the surviving record as much as about the organization.

  1. 1983

    The Herald’s opening issue reported that the student escort service would resume.

  2. 1985

    Student government defeated a campus lighting proposal in the last week of the autumn semester.

  3. 1987

    The congress passed a crosswalk resolution; screens for dormitory windows filled much of the same autumn agenda.

  4. 1993

    Bill 93-6-F proposed buying and installing emergency telephones on campus.

  5. 1995

    Two more emergency phones went in at the start of the year.

  6. 1996

    “Kristen Miller Makes Plan to Drive Party-Goers Home” is the earliest indexed trace of what became Provide-A-Ride.

  7. 2000

    Provide-A-Ride ran free on Thursday and Friday nights, a fifteen-passenger van answering calls in about fifteen minutes.

  8. 2001

    929 riders over twelve nights between 20 September and 9 November.

  9. 2011

    A surprise $15,000 retroactive Provide-A-Ride bill took $7,500 from organizational aid and $7,500 from scholarships.

  10. 2015

    Bill 10-15-S put $825.95 into a surveillance camera by the Downing Student Union cash machines, passing 15-5.

  11. 2016

    SafeWalk launched, pairing students who felt unsafe with student escorts.

  12. 2017

    Safe Ride lost its Thursday service as ridership fell from about 5,000 in autumn 2015 to nearly 1,500 in autumn 2016. Richey blamed Uber’s arrival in Bowling Green.

  13. 2022

    Three crosswalk resolutions passed in a single night.

  14. 2024

    450 Uber vouchers worth $10 each, handed out through giveaways, meeting attendance and need-based scholarships.

  15. 2026

    Resolution 7-26-S backed a crosswalk at Alumni Avenue and Kentucky Street.

How it changed. The 1980s ask was a resolution asking the university to install something. The 1990s ask was hardware SGA lobbied for and then counted, one phone at a time. From 1996 it stopped asking and ran the service itself, and by 2011 that was costing it real money. By 2024 the answer was to subsidise a commercial service instead, and to go back to crosswalks, where the 1987 congress started.

Parking, the shuttle and getting around campus1972–2026 · 14 instances

Fees, spaces, routes, shelters and fines. The clearest register in the archive of a campus physically outgrowing itself.

  1. 1972

    The regents approved a five dollar vehicle registration fee; the same issue reported students questioning it.

  2. 1979

    One of the outgoing Thornton administration’s final acts was a pair of resolutions on campus housing and parking policy.

  3. 1983

    206 students voted for a campus shuttle service.

  4. 1985

    The congress discussed running a shuttle from campus to the mall.

  5. 1988

    ASG sought shelters and seats for the campus shuttle stops.

  6. 1991

    Resolution 91-2-F asked for a one-way fare on the Big Red Shuttle for students who did not ride daily.

  7. 1994

    Expanded shuttle service, a route SGA had pushed to extend, drew enthusiasm from shops along the new stops.

  8. 1997

    A speed hump for the Regents Avenue lot and fifteen-minute parking spaces, among at least fifteen resolutions passed in a fortnight.

  9. 2002

    Bedo pressed the university after the Forest Park Trailer Park purchase collapsed and the Diddle Arena renovation removed about 200 spaces.

  10. 2003

    $8,000 asked for a shuttle shelter at the Jones-Jaggers stop.

  11. 2014

    A commuter focus group asked for an iWKU function listing free spaces in each lot, and a South Campus park and ride.

  12. 2015

    SGA backed a roughly 500-space, $10 million structure on part of Creason Lot, funded by a $30-a-semester tuition rise.

  13. 2024

    Resolution 18-23-S supported capping student parking fees at $50, noting citations ran from $10 to $600; a companion bill set aside $700 to pay other students’ fines.

  14. 2026

    The parking director told the senate the monthly diesel bill for WKU buses, normally seven to eight thousand dollars, had reached fourteen.

How it changed. In 1972 students questioned a $5 vehicle registration fee. In 1983 two hundred of them voted for the idea of a shuttle at all. By 1991 the shuttle existed and the fight was over its fare. By 2015 SGA was voting to raise its own tuition to build a garage, and by 2024 it was capping the fines and paying them for students who could not.

The grievance never changed and the object never stopped changing shape: a physical book swap, then a bulletin board in Grise Hall, then a dot-com link on the SGA website, then a cash subsidy matched by the bookstore, then two iPads, then a shelf of used Colonnade texts, then an argument about whether an inclusive-access programme should default to opt-out.

Housing and the residence halls1968–2026 · 15 instances

Who may be in the building, what is in the building, who the building is for, and finally whether the building should still be standing.

  1. 1968

    A committee investigating women’s dormitory rules ran in the same issue as a tuition increase and the Frankfort lobbying for a student regent.

  2. 1970

    The congress passed a resolution on the women’s curfew; the paper reported abolition was unlikely that year and called the curfew obsolete.

  3. 1972

    A Mass Action Committee called for a strike over dormitory visitation in the same week next year’s officers were installed.

  4. 1973

    An ASG housing survey urged improvement in dormitory conditions.

  5. 1977

    ASG and the Interhall Council backed a coed dormitory and the end of the door-ajar visitation rule.

  6. 1979

    ASG rejected a move to close Diddle Hall.

  7. 1980

    Having voted them down in September, ASG re-endorsed dormitory room inspections in a session the paper said it had nearly slept through.

  8. 1981

    Resolution 80-32 asked for change machines in the residence halls.

  9. 1986

    Bill 86-26-S asked for chains on Pearce-Ford Tower windows so they could be opened. In February 1990 the congress was still asking.

  10. 2011

    Ransdell told senators that ending the two-year on-campus living requirement did not work “from a business standpoint” while debt remained on renovations.

  11. 2017

    Housing and Residence Life’s ten-year plan made Southwest honors housing and Northeast international housing, with no mention of the integration SGA had endorsed 27-1.

  12. 2018

    The senate voted 28-2 to make the sexual orientation question on housing applications optional and add one asking whether an applicant was comfortable rooming with an LGBTQ student.

  13. 2021

    SGA reported working with Housing and Residence Life on mould in the residence halls.

  14. 2025

    Caboni told the senate that community showers and bathrooms would be phased out as halls were replaced.

  15. 2026

    Sitting beside the faculty regent, Robinson told the Faculty Senate to take a field trip and see the state of the halls.

How it changed. Three distinct eras. Rules, from 1968 to about 1985: women’s dormitory rules, curfews, visitation, coed halls, room inspections. Amenities, through the 1980s and 1990s: cable, change machines, ice machines, sprinklers, windows that open. Then belonging and fabric, from 2011: the live-on requirement used as a bargaining chip, integration, rooming questions, mould, failing air conditioning and buildings scheduled for demolition.

Tuition, fees and the road to Frankfort1968–2026 · 15 instances

Opposing the price of attending Western, lobbying the state about it, and — once the presidency and the student regent seat merged — voting on it.

  1. 1968

    A tuition increase ran in the same Herald issue as the lobbying for a student seat on the Board of Regents.

  2. 1977

    Bill 9 formally opposed a proposed tuition increase, in the same March meeting that created a Complaint Committee for student grievances.

  3. 1981

    ASG asked the state to evaluate higher education, in a legislative season dominated by budget worries.

  4. 1991

    In a single February issue ASG wanted to cap tuition increases, proposed overhauling the Detrex registration system and planned lobbying to protect financial aid.

  5. 1992

    The University Senate adopted Resolution R92.11 commending president Joe Rains for joining a Frankfort rally against cuts.

  6. 2000

    The Board of Regents voted 8-2 to raise the $16 athletics fee by $80 in two instalments. Student regent Cassie Martin was one of the two dissenters.

  7. 2001

    Bedo told the board an August vote sent students a message, then joined the unanimous vote for a 9 per cent increase.

  8. 2003

    About 200 students from six public universities rallied at the Capitol. Sears said seventeen took the initiative to get on the bus.

  9. 2004

    The congress approved $650 for transport and reserved 47 seats for a February rally, under slogans about not balancing the budget on students’ backs.

  10. 2006

    The regents approved a $46-a-semester construction fee 9-1 with Katie Dawson abstaining, and rejected her proposal to exempt the next year’s seniors 10-1.

  11. 2007

    SGA ran Walk Out Western, a class walkout aimed at Frankfort. Ransdell said he could not condone it.

  12. 2008

    Student regent Jeanne Johnson seconded a motion to cap the increase at 6 per cent. It died with two votes and the board approved 9.

  13. 2012

    SGA cancelled the $1,175 bus to the Frankfort rally after eight people confirmed, and arranged carpools instead.

  14. 2016

    Senators lobbied eight state officials in Frankfort against cuts that could have taken as much as 9 per cent of WKU’s funding.

  15. 2026

    At his last board meeting Robinson cast the only vote against a $204 increase, the sixth consecutive yearly rise.

How it changed. The 1970s and 1980s form was a resolution. The 1990s added the bus. The 2000s industrialised it, with reserved seats and a transport budget, and then began to doubt it: by 2012 the bus was cancelled when eight people signed up. Meanwhile the same student who wrote the resolution against the increase now sat on the board that passed it, and lost, usually alone.

Scholarships and organizational aid1979–2026 · 12 instances

Money going out of the door: grants to student organizations and scholarships to individual students. The largest discretionary line in the modern budget, and the thing the organization most reliably points to when asked what it does.

  1. 1979

    Resolution 79-10 urged the university to assign staff to administer academic scholarships.

  2. 2006

    About $24,000 of the previous year’s $115,000 budget had gone unspent back into WKU’s general fund; the administrative vice president added almost $10,000 more to organizational aid.

  3. 2010

    The scholarship budget rose from $12,000 to $20,000 and general senate funding fell from $20,000 to $12,000 to pay for it.

  4. 2012

    About $34,000 in organizational aid reached roughly 50 organizations.

  5. 2016

    Bill 4-16-F created SGA’s first scholarships for students with disabilities, passing unanimously after a sophomore told senators his ADHD and dyslexia had cost him merit money.

  6. 2016

    $750, matched by $100, created the Jonesville Memorial Scholarship for the Black community WKU bought and displaced.

  7. 2018

    A recurring $2,000 first-generation scholarship passed 31-1.

  8. 2019

    A record 398 applications: 132 winter term, 122 first-generation, 81 study abroad, 31 scholar development, 10 for the Intercultural Student Engagement Center and 22 for a revived Earn-a-Computer scheme.

  9. 2022

    $15,500 across five scholarship programmes, including a first $500 for Black Student Alliance awards, alongside $8,000 of organizational aid at $500 a group.

  10. 2024

    A $100,000 budget carrying $20,000 for organizational aid and $23,500 for scholarships.

  11. 2025

    295 scholarship applications in one spring.

  12. 2026

    $10,000 split among 33 organizations in grants of $100 to $500.

How it changed. It began as a request that somebody else do it. By the mid-2000s SGA was the granting body, and its categories tracked whoever the campus had decided it was neglecting: study abroad, disability, the displaced Jonesville community, first-generation students, international students. Demand outran the money. About forty applications a year became 398 in 2019 and 295 in a single spring in 2025.

Grading1983–2020 · 9 instances

The scale itself and the mechanics around it: incompletes, the withdrawal deadline, the ten-point scale, plus and minus grades, value-added grading and finally pass/fail.

  1. 1983

    ASG passed its proposal on the deadline for incomplete grades after tabling it the week before.

  2. 1984

    Its proposal for a new grade scale was denied, the first setback of the autumn.

  3. 1994

    Resolution 94-11-F asked that students receive a grade before the withdrawal deadline so they could decide whether to drop.

  4. 1997

    Resolution 97-6-F called for the ten-point grade scale to be reinstated.

  5. 2003

    SGA unanimously passed legislation against the plus/minus system proposed in the University Senate. By November its petition had passed 1,500 signatures.

  6. 2007

    Provost Barbara Burch told the University Senate she would not implement the plus/minus system it had passed 36-23. President Jeanne Johnson credited SGA’s campaign.

  7. 2012

    Provost Gordon Emslie pitched value-added grading to SGA; the senate rejected the resolution in a close vote that February.

  8. 2013

    A plus-minus resolution was tabled with the idea floated of putting the question straight to students on the spring ballot.

  9. 2020

    After the provost’s office refused and a petition passed 3,500 signatures in three days, students could take a pass in place of a B or C.

How it changed. Early legislation was procedural and small. From 2003 the category became a defensive war, and a finer grading scale kept coming back under a new name: a University Senate proposal in 2003, which Provost Barbara Burch declined to implement in 2007; Provost Gordon Emslie’s value-added scheme in 2012; a plus-minus resolution in 2013. In 2020 the polarity reversed. Instead of resisting a grading change SGA campaigned for one, and won.

    ASG proposals asked for more library hours, in the same issue that pushed the spring elections back a week.

  2. 1989

    Extended hours pursued through signed correspondence between president Amos Gott and three named administrators.

    A bill extended library hours during finals week, passed the same day as the VarsityBooks.com deal.

    The senate approved $600 to keep the library open until 2 a.m. during finals. President Colton Jessie said continuing the service was never in question.

  5. 2011

  6. 2025

  5. 2006

    SGA took up a $45-a-semester fee to pay for a campus-wide wireless network, a health centre and renovations.

  6. 2006

  11. 2026

    WKU’s new Artificial Intelligence Committee was in early talks about bulk subscriptions for all students.

How it changed. Cable television gave way to photocopiers and email accounts, then typewriters, then a printing quota, then a wireless network, then streaming video, then iPads, charging stations, second-hand computers, graphing calculators and finally bulk subscriptions to commercial artificial intelligence. The consistent move was SGA buying, on a small scale, the device gap it could see.

Evaluating the teaching1969–2021 · 8 instances

Student evaluation of faculty, courses, teaching assistants and advisors. Repeatedly won, repeatedly lost, repeatedly restarted.

  1. 1969

    An ASG committee compiled the results of Western’s first student teacher-evaluation effort, after the paper had editorialised demanding the tabulation move faster.

  2. 1972

    The Herald judged ASG’s course evaluation the best yet, as filing opened for autumn offices.

  3. 1987

    ASG took up a proposal to make course evaluations a requirement.

  4. 1989

    January efforts to find a way to print the teacher evaluations preceded a recommendation for an extra study day before exams.

  5. 2000

    Resolution 00-2-S asked the Faculty Senate to approve a mandatory, published SGA-sponsored evaluation from that autumn.

  6. 2004

A mandatory published evaluation was won on paper in 2000, scaled back to five questions on TopNet by 2004, extended to teaching assistants in 2013 and to academic advisors in 2021.

How it changed. It started as individual fights the record holds mostly as headlines. From the mid-1990s the mechanism shifted to policy, and from 2013 to guaranteed seats. The 2016-17 peak drew national coverage and a flat rejection; the 2025 amendment ran the other way, renaming the diversity committee under a federal executive order.

Two

What people said about it

Sixty years of verdicts, grouped by the kind of verdict rather than by the year. The College Heights Herald is the principal witness for most of this period and the standing antagonist, and it opened with a judgement rather than a welcome. SGA’s own legislation files, the Board of Regents minutes and the university’s records carry the rest.

The register moves in phases. From 1966 to about 1980 the paper scolds the student body for not deserving its government. From 1980 to 1999 it stops arguing and starts drawing, and the presidents write back in the letters column. After 1999 no editorial cartoon about SGA appears in this archive at all, and the criticism migrates from the opinion page into the room itself: students standing up at meetings, organizations withdrawing recognition, and censure hearings.

The apathy argument

One continuous thread, and the only one in the archive that can be read as a series of numbers. The counts the record preserves, in order.

The organization has spent sixty years measuring itself against the day it was created. 2,538 students voted in the founding referendum of April 1966, and 2,894 in the election of 1968 — about 34 per cent of a roughly 8,500-student enrolment. Enrolment then more than doubled and the raw count never recovered. Against the 17,500 students reported in 2002 and about 18,000 in 2004, the modern ceiling is roughly 2,400.

Three cautions before reading the series as a trend. The denominators change and are recorded only at scattered points, so the modern counts cannot be turned into comparable percentages. Autumn senate elections and spring executive elections are different animals: autumn totals in the record run from 398 to 977, spring general-election totals from 908 to 2,894. And some figures are ballots cast while others are votes in the presidential race, which is why 2018 appears here as 2,378. Three totals below — 2002, 2005 and 2010 — are the two candidates’ votes added together, because the record gives no overall figure for those years. The long gap in the middle is real: between the general election of April 1992 and the collapsed election of 1999, not one race in this archive carries a vote total.

Where it jumped, and what caused it

Every upward spike in the series is attached to one of three things: a constitutional moment, a referendum sharing the ballot, or a genuinely contested race. 1966 and 1968 were votes about whether the organization should exist and who should run it. In 2003 the presidency was uncontested but a $3 radio-station fee was on the ballot, and the executive vice president who championed it had argued in as many words that it would boost turnout. In 2006 SGA deliberately ran its Division I-A football referendum alongside the homecoming queen election, and the president conceded the tie-in probably drew football-friendly voters. 2016 and 2018 were real contests. So was 2026.

The counterexamples point the same way. Turnout fell to 908 in 2014, in a year the sitting president ran unopposed and, she said, spent nothing on her campaign. The presidency was uncontested in 2023, 2024 and 2025. Then in April 2026 two tickets took 27 student-submitted questions at a town hall, sat for a debate with the campus radio station, and 1,601 students voted — 635 more than the year before, a rise of 66 per cent. The archive’s own verdict is that turnout tracks whether there is an actual contest.

What the organization tried instead fills sixty years: a survey of wanted activities in 1967, extra polling places in 1972, published roll-call votes in 1978, a telephone campaign in 1986, a ban on write-in campaigns in 1988, Anti-Apathy Week in 1990, an ambassador council in 2003, extra credit for attending the debate in 2008, new seats for constituencies that had none, an SGA clause in every course syllabus in 2022, Uber vouchers in 2024, and a POLLapalooza in 2025 that fell 2,034 votes short of its stated goal. Two moves in the same decade cut in opposite directions on the same problem: in 2017 the senate lowered its own grade-point requirement from 2.5 to 2.0 to make the body more representative, ratified by referendum, and in 2024 senators voted to put the floors back up.

Four

The recurring fights

Inside its own walls the organization can do almost anything: void an election, disqualify a president-elect, censure a vice president, remove three senators in one vote. Outside them it has rarely compelled anything, and the exceptions came by persuading somebody who could act.

The sharpest artefact of that ceiling is 2013. SGA’s Judicial Council disqualified the president-elect 3-2; she appealed to the vice president for student affairs; he reinstated her; and the council concluded in an emergency meeting that it did not believe it had the power to contest him, while recording its official disapproval. The student judiciary discovered it was not final.

What SGA wins outside itself, it wins by producing evidence somebody else can act on: 1,500 signatures against plus/minus grading in 2003, a 1,066-765 referendum that sent a fee to the regents that same spring, 3,500 signatures in three days in 2020. Not by passing a resolution.

The direction of travel reversed repeatedly, which is itself the finding. Caps went up in 2017 and were halved in 2019.

The formal disciplinary machinery, used against senators, justices, vice presidents and once against a sitting president. There is a near-total documentary gap between 1976 and 2004.

  1. 1976

    Congress member Gerard Faulk was acquitted at impeachment, in the same issue that reported the presidential election result.

  2. 1987

    ASG moved to get tough on absenteeism after a member lost his seat.

  3. 2004

    Articles of impeachment were drafted against the outgoing finance vice president after $611 was found missing. The council ruled the two-week process could not finish before he left the office anyway.

  4. 2009

    The Judicial Council removed a senator for excessive unexcused absences; he did not attend to defend himself.

  5. 2011

    Ten of 36 senators were called before the judicial board for absences. Two were dismissed for failing to attend their own hearings; one was reappointed by the president three months later.

  6. 2015

    The council voted unanimously not to censure a senator over an “impeach Ransdell” sign, finding his conduct protected by the First Amendment. The chief justice resigned days later.

  7. 2023

    The Speaker of the Senate requested a censure hearing against her own president. The council voted unanimously against censure; a Title IX report seeking his removal was filed two days later.

  8. 2024

    The council voted 7-0 to remove three senators, each with at least six unexcused absences, under the provision requiring removal after a second censure.

How it changed. The first documented case was an acquittal. For nearly thirty years afterwards the archive records no impeachment at all, and discipline appeared only as enforcement against absence. It returned in 2004 as articles that died on a technicality, and from 2009 became routine and bureaucratic, until a 2024 amendment made two censures in one year automatic grounds for removal.

Vetoes and overrides1981–2019 · 6 instances

The executive striking down what the senate has passed, and the senate’s usually failed attempt to strike back.

  1. 1981

    ASG vetoed a plan to pay an education lobbyist, weeks after rallying publicly for higher education funding.

  2. 1991

    Bill #91-6-S rewrote the by-law on overriding a presidential veto; the new procedure was tested weeks later when the emergency-kit resolution survived a veto.

  3. 1996

    A resolution was vetoed in the same week SGA proposed more campus lighting. The archive does not name it.

  4. 2014

    Resolution 11-14-F, supporting a smoke-free campus, passed 14-10 and was vetoed after the meeting. The override failed at the last meeting of the semester.

  5. 2017

    A resolution that had passed the senate unanimously was vetoed as unconstitutional. Its author resigned the same night.

  6. 2019

    The executive board vetoed the Alpha Xi Delta resolution 4-0 with two abstentions, and the senate sustained the veto.

How it changed. Before 2004 the archive records vetoes only as isolated index lines. After the three-branch constitution the veto became a live weapon used against the senate’s most contested votes, and the override became the senate’s most reliable failure.

Quorum failures and empty seats1975–2025 · 8 instances

Meetings that could not be held, votes that failed for want of bodies, and senators removed or resigned for absence. The internal face of the apathy argument.

  1. 1975

    ASG action was halted twice in one week for lack of quorum. An editorial said the confusion signalled a need for change.

  2. 1987

    A constitutional amendment was defeated because too few members turned up to vote on it.

  3. 2006

    With 16 of 31 senators present the senate lacked quorum and tabled two resolutions.

  4. 2014

    An amendment that had passed was voided when the Executive Council found too few senators had attended for the vote to count.

  5. 2016

    The Speaker told the senate that eleven at-large seats should have been filled the previous spring but only ten were, leaving SGA a senator short all summer.

  6. 2019

    Nine senators were forced to step down at once, leaving the chamber depleted with no new legislation introduced.

  7. 2020

    Senators and committee chairs had quietly vacated their positions since the autumn; two committee chairs were empty.

  8. 2025

    The meeting to pass the DEI amendment initially lacked the 21 members needed for a two-thirds vote, so absent members were phoned onto Zoom to reach quorum.

How it changed. Early instances were reported as one-off embarrassments in the Herald. From the 2000s SGA’s own minutes recorded quorum failures directly, and by the 2020s the Judicial Council had codified machinery for removing absentees.

Money that cannot be accounted for1975–2024 · 8 instances

Lost concert money, missing funds, unspent budgets, retroactive bills, and the scholarships SGA awards to its own members.

  1. 1975

    A September concert drew 4,300 people and lost $7,000.

  2. 1976

    The Seals and Crofts concert set the organization back $3,800.

  3. 2002

    Three members petitioned congress over $28,000 they said was unaccounted for. President Jamie Sears said she had absolutely no idea where the money went.

  4. 2002

    By October the $13,000 had been traced: it had slipped back into the general fund and been spent on other things, including computers for the SGA office.

  5. 2006

    About $24,000 of a $115,000 budget went unspent and returned to the university’s general fund.

  6. 2013

    The senate stripped out the bylaw that would have barred serving SGA members from SGA-sponsored scholarships.

  7. 2015

    A former senator published a letter arguing a precedent set that session had damaged the organizational aid process.

  8. 2024

    A 6-0 censure of the administrative vice president for spending a $425.25 deposit before the funding bill passed.

How it changed. In the 1970s the problem was operational: SGA ran concerts and lost on them. From 2002 it became accountability failure — money that vanished into the general fund, purchases with no receipts, a surprise bill for a predecessor’s contract. By the 2010s the fight was over whether SGA members might take SGA’s own scholarship money.

Fighting the Board of Regents1991–2026 · 8 instances

Direct confrontation with WKU’s governing board, over its chairman, its contracts, its fees and its process. The place where SGA’s formal representation and its actual power are furthest apart.

  1. 1991

    Resolution 91-6-F asked that Joe Iracane not be re-elected chair, citing federal investigations. The board re-elected him a week later.

  2. 2000

    The board raised the athletics fee 8-2. Student regent Cassie Martin was one of two dissenters.

  3. 2006

    The construction fee passed 9-1 with the student regent abstaining; her proposal to exempt the next year’s seniors was rejected 10-1.

  4. 2015

    The senate voted 21-4 to disapprove of the procedure by which the Confucius Institute building was authorised. The board declined to revisit it.

  5. 2016

    The student regent published an open letter to students explaining his vote against a medical centre partnership approved 6-4-1.

  6. 2018

    A unanimous resolution sought renegotiation of a 20-year dining contract the president said had never come to SGA or to the full board.

  7. 2024

    At the regents’ retreat the student regent challenged administrators over student worker funding and afterwards called the answers cookie-cutter.

  8. 2026

    The student regent cast the only vote against a $204 tuition increase at his last meeting.

How it changed. The pattern was stable: SGA passed a resolution and the board did what it was going to do. What changed was the student regent’s posture — from voting yes under protest, through abstention, to open letters of dissent and lone recorded no votes. The dissent got louder as it stayed equally ineffective.

The administration overrules the students1972–2025 · 8 instances

SGA passes something, the administration declines it, and the organization discovers the limit of a resolution. Including the rare inverse: the campaigns that worked.

  1. 1972

    ASG rejected the university president’s position on an Office of Black Affairs.

  2. 2003

    The petition against plus/minus grading passed 1,500 signatures. Four years later the provost declined to implement the system.

  3. 2011

    The senate voted 19-8 against renaming Downing University Center. The Administrative Council decided it would be called Downing Student Union anyway.

  4. 2016

    Administrators changed the pub name students had chosen. The president said it made SGA question how much the administration listens to students.

  5. 2016

    Ransdell declined the meningitis vaccination requirement the senate had passed unanimously.

  6. 2017

    Housing and Residence Life’s ten-year plan made no mention of the integration SGA had endorsed 27-1.

  7. 2020

    After a petition of more than 3,500 signatures in three days and a 40-9 Faculty Senate vote, the provost reversed the university’s position on Pass/D/Fail. It is the clearest win in the record against a decision already made.

  8. 2025

    General Counsel told the senate that state law now bound SGA’s spending, that its funding had to be content neutral, and that the Pride Center would lose its office.

How it changed. Early confrontations were about campus life and rights. From the 2000s they became academic and contractual, and the pattern hardened into a formula: SGA passed a resolution, an administrator answered by email or letter, and the answer was no. The wins came when SGA supplied a number an administrator could act on, not when it passed a resolution.

Whether it is allowed to have opinions about the world1969–2025 · 7 instances

The recurring argument about temperament: is student government a service organization or a political one? It surfaces whenever the senate takes a position beyond the campus fence.

  1. 1969

    The Associated Student Congress endorsed the Vietnam Moratorium; the paper called it a bold step.

  2. 1982

    ASG endorsed a congressional candidate; five days later the paper editorialised that endorsing was not wise for the organization.

  3. 2017

    The reparations resolution passed 19-10-1 and drew national coverage. Both authors described it as a conversation starter.

  4. 2017

    Resolution 2-17-F urging a clean DREAM Act failed 12-17, one senator saying it was not the university’s place to put a political hat in the ring.

  5. 2021

    A resolution condemning bans on the teaching of critical race theory passed 16-15 with two abstentions after weeks of debate.

  6. 2024

    Bill 50-23-S, reaffirming SGA as nonpartisan and stating a duty to put forward only nonpartisan legislation, passed unanimously.

  7. 2025

    The diversity committee was renamed under a federal executive order, ratified by 88 per cent of voters.

How it changed. The archive dates the origin to 1969 and the Vietnam Moratorium endorsement, and the same argument returned in 1982, 2017 and 2021. It never settled. In 2017 a clean DREAM Act resolution failed and a narrower solidarity resolution passed two weeks later by staying inside a jurisdiction the senate agreed it had. By 2024 the senate voted unanimously to declare itself nonpartisan.

Five

How the organization changed shape

Across sixty years SGA rewrote its own constitution constantly and gained almost no new power by doing it.

Almost every structural change that enlarged what the organization could do came from outside its own document: a 1968 state statute that created the student regent seat, a 1982 state bill that changed how the seat was filled, the university’s annual decision about the budget line, and in 2025 a state law that told SGA what it may spend money on. The internal rewrites — from the revision fights of 1969 to 1972, through the 2004 Constitutional Convention, to the codified bylaws of 2026 — almost always redistributed authority among students rather than adding any against the administration.

Two items on the list below did come from inside. The referendum mechanism first used in 2003 put a $3 radio fee to students and sent it to the regents, and was used again for the Talisman fee and the football question. And the 2004 convention made every enrolled student a member of SGA with the right to vote in its elections, the quietest clause in the record. 132 of roughly 18,000 students voted on the constitution that did it.

The name changed once in sixty years1966–2025 · 7 instances

Founded as the Associated Students of Western Kentucky University, renamed the Student Government Association only in 1992. Two earlier attempts to change the branding failed outright.

  1. 1966

    President Kelly Thompson approved the constitution of the Associated Students on 1 April; students ratified it on 26 April.

  2. 1980

    ASG proposed a title change. Nothing in the archive shows it taking effect.

  3. 1981

    A resolution asked for a new logo; the congress voted to keep the one it had a week later.

  4. 1992

    The Associated Students voted to rename themselves the Student Government Association; students ratified it a week later and it took effect that autumn.

  5. 1992

    The August minutes appear under both titles. The organization was still in transition at its first autumn meeting.

  6. 2016

    MyCampusToo was made a permanent standing committee and simultaneously renamed the Committee for Diversity and Inclusion.

  7. 2025

    Bill 21-25-S renamed the Diversity, Equity and Inclusion Committee the Action and Opportunity Committee and retitled its officers.

How it changed. After 1992 the renaming energy moved down to committee and officer titles, where it has stayed. The most recent rename, in 2025, was a response to a federal executive order and its co-author called the change mostly a formality.

The constitution as a permanent construction site1969–2026 · 10 instances

Constitutional revision is the single most repeated activity in the entire record. There is a revision committee, a referendum, an amendment package or a full rewrite in more years than not, from the organization’s third year to its last.

  1. 1969

    A committee was appointed to study revision of the constitution, three years in.

  2. 1972

    The congress voted section by section on a rewritten constitution, with the Herald printing the full proposed executive-branch text. The archive does not show the referendum’s result or turnout.

  3. 1978

    The congress approved a revised constitution.

  4. 1983

    Changes were approved in March; the Dean of Students publicly questioned them the following week; students voted on them on the same April ballot that elected Jack Smith.

  5. 1986

    Twenty years of amendments were consolidated into one document, the only surviving snapshot of how the 1966 framework had been patched.

  6. 2004

    The Constitutional Convention rebuilt the body into three branches. 132 students voted on it.

  7. 2005

    A package of 40 constitutional amendments passed 1,193 to 262.

  8. 2013

    The senate went through the constitution article by article, replacing class-year seats with college seats and guaranteeing seats for the regional campuses.

  9. 2023

    Three Legislative Operations bills corrected the governing documents, including references to a University Senate that had since split in two.

  10. 2026

    The 25th Senate adopted a codified version of the Executive and Legislative Bylaws, to supersede all prior versions.

How it changed. The early rewrites were fought section by section in public, with the paper printing the proposed text. From the 1980s they became routine housekeeping. The 2004 convention was the only true rebuild. After it the pattern was annual amendment packages, and by the 2020s the work was largely custodial: fixing references to bodies that no longer existed.

The student regent seat, made and remade by statute1967–2009 · 10 instances

The largest single expansion of student power in the record was written in Frankfort, not in the SGA constitution. The seat was created by state legislation, carried no vote for years, was separately elected for most of its first two decades, then merged into the presidency.

  1. 1967

    A Herald article reported support for putting a student on the Board of Regents, months before a bill reached committee.

  2. 1968

    William Menser assumed duties as a board member. The seat carried no vote.

  3. 1969

    Because president Bill Straeffer was an out-of-state student, the Congress elected Paul Gerard to the seat instead. He sat as a non-voting member.

  4. 1970

    The legislature dropped the bill that would have given the seat a vote, under a headline saying lawmakers had kept students impotent.

  5. 1972

    “Student Regent May Get Vote.” Four years after the seat was created it was still non-voting.

  6. 1974

    Gregory McKinney was sworn in as WKU’s first African American student regent.

  7. 1982

    After a state bill changed how the regent was chosen, five students stood in the first campus-wide election for the seat. No majority on 9 February forced a runoff; the resulting term ran about two months.

  8. 1983

    President Margaret Ragan published a piece explaining that the presidency and the student regent seat were now separate positions.

  9. 1991

    WKU’s own newsletter reported Heather Falmlen as both student regent and ASG president — the two offices, separately elected two decades earlier, held by one person.

  10. 2009

    After a resignation split the offices, the Judicial Council voted 2-1 to hold a special election over the chief justice’s dissent. The seat had sat empty 26 days.

How it changed. Created non-voting in 1968 and filled by the Congress. Campaigns for a vote failed repeatedly into the 1970s. A 1982 state bill changed how the regent was chosen and produced WKU’s first campus-wide regent election. By 1991 one person held both offices, which is why every presidential resignation since has been a governance problem.

Senate size and who counts as a constituency1968–2025 · 11 instances

The chamber has been called a Congress and a Senate, has run from a handful of at-large congressmen to 50 members, and has repeatedly redefined which students get a guaranteed seat.

  1. 1968

    The elected slate was president, vice president, secretary, treasurer and two congressmen at large.

  2. 1979

    A seats bill passed after being revised under pressure; the paper called the representation bill a small step in the right direction.

  3. 1983

    Twenty-four students were elected to the Senate — the only hard seat count the archive preserves for the whole of the 1980s.

  4. 1988

    The organization publicly explained its empty seats; four congress positions had been filled at a single meeting.

  5. 1990

    The congress voted to expand itself so that classes and student groups would get more voice.

  6. 2006

    The senate opened the year with 18 seats and eight senators present, swore in eight more that night, and had grown to 31 seats by November.

  7. 2011

    The Herald reported 50 total members and an operating budget of $121,335 drawn from tuition.

  8. 2013

    A referendum created an international student senate seat, which the president said would make WKU the first student government in the state to have one.

  9. 2018

    A bill replacing at-large and graduate seats with first-generation, transfer, non-traditional and intercultural seats failed 14-18.

  10. 2023

    Bill 14-23-F created a Mahurin Honors College senator. Its first occupant was elected student body president in 2026.

  11. 2025

    The spring ballot filled at-large, class, transfer, non-traditional, first-generation, intercultural and college seats — constituencies the 1966 constitution had no concept of.

How it changed. From four executives and two congressmen at large in 1968, through residence hall and college representation in the late 1970s and 1980s, to a 2013 rebuild that replaced class-year seats with college seats and guaranteed the regional campuses, to a 36-seat constitutional formula and then a proliferation of identity and status seats: international, Gatton Academy, graduate, first-generation, transfer, Honors.

What actually changed the organization’s reach1967–2025 · 8 instances

A short list. Each of these altered what SGA could actually do, and almost every one originated outside SGA’s own constitution.

  1. 1967

    Dean Keown’s memo proposing a student fee structure to create a budget. Money made the body operational, and it was proposed by the Dean of Students, not by students.

  2. 1968

    The state statute creating the student regent seat gave students a permanent chair in the room where tuition and fees are set. SGA neither wrote it nor could amend it.

  3. 1982

    The state regents bill changed how the student regent was chosen, producing the first campus-wide election for the seat.

  4. 2003

    The first student referendum in SGA history put a $3 radio fee to students and sent it to the regents. The mechanism was used again for the Talisman fee and the football question.

  5. 2004

    The Constitutional Convention made every enrolled student a member of SGA with the right to vote in its elections. It is the quietest clause in the record and the one that changed the organization’s standing most.

  6. 2011

    SGA had to pass a resolution on the Downing renovation’s design and fees before the regents could approve the project; the senate tabled it in December for lack of student input. A veto point that actually bit.

  7. 2020

    Pass/D/Fail was won by mobilisation rather than by any clause: a petition of more than 3,500 signatures in three days and nearly twenty pages of student testimony.

  8. 2025

    House Bill 4 bound SGA’s spending because SGA receives university money. A genuine reduction in its discretion, imposed entirely from outside.

How it changed. The record’s hardest finding about itself: sixty years of internal constitutional revision redistributed authority among students, and the changes that enlarged or reduced the organization’s actual reach came from a state statute, a state bill, a university budget line, a referendum mechanism and, in 2025, a state law.

Reform that was cosmetic1977–2025 · 7 instances

Changes framed as structural reform that left the organization’s capacity untouched: renamings, symbolic committees, procedural tinkering and self-evaluations with no recorded follow-through.

  1. 1977

    Bill 6 proposed that ASG conduct a self-evaluation for transparency and improvement. The archive holds the bill and nothing about a result.

  2. 1981

    The proposed title change and the new-logo resolution both went nowhere.

  3. 1985

    The congress abolished its own Finance Committee. It wrote a Financial Advisory Council into the by-laws the following April: the same function under a new name.

  4. 2014

    An amendment stripping the president’s sole power to appoint justices passed, then was undone by the same attendance problem it was meant to address.

  5. 2018

    Moving committee-chair appointments from the president to the speaker failed 26-2, with the speaker himself voting against. It had failed the year before too.

  6. 2021

    A bill barred the Speaker of the Senate from voting on legislation, alongside two bills scrubbing obsolete 2007-era bylaws.

  7. 2025

    The DEI committee rename. A co-author called the change mostly a formality; the chair said the committee at its core was not changing.

How it changed. The pattern did not change. The clearest single case was 1985-86, when the congress abolished its Finance Committee in September and wrote a Financial Advisory Council into the by-laws the following April — the same function under a new name, seven months apart.

The budget, and what could be done with it1973–2024 · 8 instances

The total is set by the university. What SGA controls is how it is divided, and in several years how much of it goes unspent.

  1. 1973

    The Board of Regents voted to increase the ASG allotment, and the organization became something else: a concert promoter, a survey-taker, a runner of free evening courses.

  2. 1979

    ASG faced losing control of student activities funding, and lost it. On 31 March the Board of Regents gave the lectures and concerts to a rebuilt University Center Board and funded that board with $80,000 for programming.

  3. 1986

    A $12,100 budget, reported alongside a plan to reach students by telephone.

  4. 2004

    With about $45,000 left of a $105,000 budget, SGA moved to spend the remainder before leftover funds went to offset university cuts.

  5. 2006

    About $24,000 of $115,000 had gone unspent the previous year and returned to the general fund.

  6. 2013

    A $125,000 budget funded from tuition at about $6.25 a student, after a planned increase did not come through.

  7. 2015

    The budget fell $19,500 to $138,500 when a private donation was not repeated and the university cut a further $4,500. Organizational funding took the largest share of the loss.

  8. 2024

    $100,000, the same total as the year before, with $20,000 for organizational aid and $23,500 for scholarships.

How it changed. ASG ran concerts and lost money on them through the 1970s, until the Regents moved the lectures and concerts to a rebuilt University Center Board in March 1979 and funded it with $80,000. University cuts in 2013 removed the automatic inflation increase SGA had been receiving, but the modern totals did not move in one direction: $125,000 in 2013-14, $138,500 in 2015-16, $100,000 in 2024-25. The recurring problem was not only scarcity but the failure to spend.

Six

Where the record is thin

The shape of these patterns is partly the shape of what survived. The gaps are findings too, and reading past them would make the recent decades look worse than the early ones simply because they are better documented.

For roughly the first twenty-five years the evidence is a digitised article index rather than article text: we know what the Herald said a piece was about, not what it said. Disciplinary conflict only becomes visible when SGA starts keeping and posting its own minutes, which is why the 1970s look less litigious than the 2010s. The 1980s produced recounts, a voided general election, a revote and a write-in dispute that ran six months, which is not the profile of an organization with no internal conflict.

  1. 1969–1975

    No general-election turnout figure survives for any year in this stretch. The constitutional referendum of March 1972 is the most consequential structural vote of the decade, and the archive does not show its result or its turnout.

  2. The 1970s

    The decade yields three raw vote counts in total — 527 in the 1976 primary, 95 in a 1978 freshman primary, 830 in the 1979 primary — and two margins, the 26 votes by which Bob Moore won in 1977 and the eleven by which a freshman president was elected in 1978. There is not one general-election total for the whole decade.

  3. 1979–80

    The emptiest presidency in the file. The year page states outright that the archive does not yet show what the Hargrove administration passed or fought over, and that Herald issues from that autumn and spring have not been checked.

  4. The 1980s

    Only one senate seat count survives for the entire decade: the 24 senators elected in March 1983. Committee structure is known from two bills and one filed Judicial Council roster. The published result of the April 1987 presidential election is not recorded at all.

  5. 1990–1998

    One turnout number in nine years. Several years’ pages state that no full officer list, committee structure or seat count survives beyond the president. The 1995 and 1996 headlines describe a decline the archive cannot quantify.

  6. 2002–03

    TopSCHOLAR’s full-text files for this year sit behind an automated-access check and have not been read. The ad hoc financial review committee formed in the gazebo dispute is the only committee identified by name.

  7. 2019–2021

    No presidential turnout figure survives for the September 2020 or April 2021 elections, and the 2019 results story reported none either. The COVID year carries no editorial criticism of SGA at all — which is a fact about the sources, not about the year.

  8. Cartoons after 1999

    No editorial cartoon about SGA appears anywhere in this archive after the 1999 cartoon on nasty campaigns. Whether the paper stopped drawing student government, or the digitised index stopped recording cartoons, cannot be resolved from this record.

  9. Questions the record drops

    No source found reports how the investigations into the regents’ chairman ended, or how SGA resolved its 1999 investigation into racially charged campaign fliers, or why Bob Moore was ruled ineligible for an SGA job in 1978.

What this is

A year-by-year record of student government at Western Kentucky University, from the ratification of the Associated Students constitution in April 1966 to the present. Every entry names the source it came from.

Corrections

The names here start with the plaque in the SGA Chambers, and the plaque is known to be wrong in places. Where the archive disagrees with it, this site follows the archive and records the change on the corrections page. If you served, or you know that a year here is wrong, the project wants to hear from you: people who held an office can be given the keys to their own year on the contribute page.

What it rests on

Everything here comes from what is publicly available online: the digitised Herald and SGA's own papers on TopSCHOLAR, the Talisman yearbooks, wkuherald.com, the university's news pages and the Wayback Machine. That is a fraction of what happened. Student government has met most weeks for sixty years, and the greater part of it was never written down, or was written down on paper that has never been scanned. A thin year here is a thin record, not a quiet one.

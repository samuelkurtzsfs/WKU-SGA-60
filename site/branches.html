The other two branches

How it was built

Student government at Western is not only its presidents. A legislature has met since 1966, first as a Congress of class and club representatives and since 2004 as a Senate of college and at-large seats, and a judicial body has ruled on impeachments, elections and eligibility since the early 1970s. This page is the history of those two, as institutions: how they were constituted, who sat in them, what they could actually do, and how each was rebuilt when a new constitution arrived.

It covers 10 periods of the legislature and 5 of the judiciary. Each account is followed by the facts it rests on, every one separately sourced, so a reader can check it rather than take it.

The legislature

1966-67 through 1969-70

The legislative branch of the Associated Students was the Student Congress, called Congress in the document that created it. The constitution printed in the College Heights Herald on 7 April 1966 vested the legislative authority of the Associated Students in Congress and set out its composition. Each of the four class councils sent its president, its vice president and one representative at large, twelve seats in all. Professional and honor groups, departmental clubs and general clubs sent one representative each. The Interfraternity Council, the Panhellenic Council, the men's residence halls and the women's residence halls sent two apiece. Two representatives were elected at large. That came to twenty-five seats. The four officers of the Associated Students served as officers of Congress and sat in it as well: the vice president, secretary and treasurer as voting members, and the president presiding, voting only to break a tie, and required to give the secretary an agenda ten days before a meeting. Officers and members took office in the second week of May and served one calendar year. Two-thirds of the total membership had to be present before Congress could consider business.

Congress gave final approval to the budget, which the Finance Committee prepared and the Executive Council approved in draft, approved constitutional appointments, and ruled on petitions from student organizations seeking University recognition, Greek-letter social fraternities excepted. It could delegate executive power to the Executive Council for fixed periods by a three-fourths vote, remove a member for just cause by a three-fourths vote on secret ballot, set its bylaws by two-thirds, and amend the constitution by two-thirds of its full membership followed by a majority of students voting in a regular election. Every change was subject to University approval. Three constitutional committees answered to it: Rules and Elections, Finance, and Student Activities, five members each, at least two of them seniors.

The roster of the first Congress kept by the President's Office lists twenty-five people. Four are the officers, so twenty-one of the twenty-five seats are accounted for: two Interfraternity and two Panhellenic representatives, two men's residence hall representatives and one woman's, one each for professional and honorary fraternities, departmental clubs and general college clubs, two campus-wide representatives at large, and three seats each for the senior, junior and sophomore classes. No freshman seat appears, and the archive does not say why. Three of the twenty-five, David Arvin, Forrest (Bucky) Lanning and Marshall Peace, are marked as members of the Executive Council, matching the three representatives the constitution had Congress elect from its own number. TopSCHOLAR's description of the roster dates the election of these officers to May 1966.

The apportionment was challenged early. The Herald reported on 20 February 1969 that Congress had voted 18 to 1 the previous week for Kent Gildersleeve's resolution to form a revision committee, and had elected Gildersleeve, junior class representative Mike Durham and sophomore class vice-president Doug Alexander to it. Gildersleeve, who held the general clubs seat, said clubs and professional and service fraternities with memberships as large as Greek organizations had no direct representative while the Panhellenic and Interfraternity Councils had two each. President Bill Straeffer backed the resolution, called the existing constitution barely adequate and said one representative stood for about forty clubs. Sophomore class president Steve Stephens cast the only vote against, saying the question had been brought up hurriedly and that members had not been told what needed changing. Straeffer was to appoint two further members, one from the Judicial Council and one from the Executive Council, the chairman of Congress could appoint more subject to Congress's approval, and the committee was capped at ten and due to report on 8 May 1969.

The Herald of 24 April 1970 reported that Congress had instead read and approved four constitutional changes at a recent session, the date of which the paper does not give. They were a change in membership and voting rights covering the right of graduate students to hold elected office, approved in a form requiring a candidate to be an undergraduate and to stay one throughout the term; the addition of two members from each of the colleges together with two more representatives at large; the establishment of inferior judicial councils; and impeachment of members of Congress. All four were to go to students on the campus cheerleader election ticket the following month. At the same session Steve Todd of the constitutional revision committee urged Congress to consider an at-large system of representation in place of the guaranteed seat system then in use, and parliamentarian Phil Meyers concurred, arguing that Greeks, dormitories and clubs did not represent the student body and that groups such as black students and veterans had little voice under it.

The earliest numbered piece of Congress legislation the archive holds for these years is Associated Students Congress Bill No. 3, the Environmental Commission, written by vice president David Porter and passed in a regular session on 14 May 1970. It created a nine-member commission of five students, two faculty and two administrators to alert university, city, state and federal agencies to problems of pollution, environment and health, and to report annually to Congress. Porter circulated the bill to faculty and administrators the next day.

The legislature

1970-71 through 1974-75

The legislative branch was the Congress. The 1971 Talisman calls it the Congress of Associated Students and records it as composed of a maximum of 43 members; the Herald called it the Associated Student Congress, and after 1972 the ASG Congress. The same volume gives its functions as establishing policies and programmes, ruling on petitions from student organisations seeking University recognition, and proposing modification of University policies affecting student activities and welfare, and describes an Executive Council of the four executive officers and three members elected from Congress, which considered policies and proposals before they went to Congress. Congress met weekly. Members were elected, the main offices in the spring, and Academic Council seats and freshman class offices in the autumn. In September 1974 the Herald reported that president Jeff Consolo named eight students to Congress seats; the archive does not record the rule under which he appointed them.

Congress rewrote its charter twice between 1970 and 1972. It endorsed a proposed constitution on 20 November 1970, and the Herald of 11 December 1970 reported that the constitution passed with 2,094 students voting, in the issue that also carried an editorial calling the referendum too much too late. A committee opened a fresh study of the constitutions in January 1971. The Constitution Revision Committee released its findings in September 1971, and the 1972 Talisman records that it presented President Dero Downing with guidelines for a new constitution that autumn. Congress voted on a section of the document on 22 February 1972, the Herald reported the executive branch accepted on 29 February, and the paper reported Congress approving the new constitution on 3 March 1972. A referendum followed on 29 and 30 March, and the Herald of 31 March reported that students had approved it. The 1973 Talisman sets out what the new constitution did: the body took the name Associated Student Government, the vice presidency was split into administrative and activities posts, and representation from the academic colleges replaced the special-interest groups previously represented, giving ten representatives at large, two from each academic college, the president and vice president of each class, and the ten Academic Council members and alternates. The archive holds no further charter work until March 1975, when the Herald reported a constitutional change approved.

Academic seats recur through the record. The Regents seated eight students on the Academic Council in August 1970. The 1973 Talisman states that 1972-73 was the first year Academic Council student members could vote on curriculum decisions; the 1972 Talisman, however, describes vice president Reginald Glass attending the Council in 1971-72 as one of two voting student members, and the archive does not resolve the conflict. For 1974-75, by president Jeff Consolo's account in the 1975 Talisman, there were two representatives from each of the University's seven colleges, half of them with voting status, out of 40 votes on the Council, and ASG drew up a resolution for the Council's Rules Committee asking for three more student votes, to make its three executive officers voting members.

Congress voted on 24 April 1973 to put a head fee increase to a student referendum; the 1974 Talisman records that the Regents instead approved an $11,000 increase in the student affairs budget allocated to ASG that August, which president Steve Yater said was better because it took no more money from students. ASG also set its own entertainment budget: the 1975 Talisman puts it at $42,000 for 1974-75, with $27,000 of that set aside for two free concerts. A bill seeking an ASG financial report was reported in the Herald of 18 October 1974, and Congress heard the treasurer explain the budget on 7 February 1975, in the issue that reported financial difficulties threatening ASG's entertainment role.

Quorum appears at both ends of the period. The Herald of 4 September 1970 reported that a failure to maintain a quorum had delayed action on key issues, and a quorum question tied to the Associated Student Congress constitution went on the ballot in October 1970. On 28 February 1975 the Herald reported ASG action halted twice that week for lack of a quorum. The archive carries nothing on quorum in the years between.

The legislature

1975-76 through 1979-80

The legislative branch of Associated Student Government was called Congress. A congress member writing in ASG's own supplement said it met weekly to hold hearings, debate and conduct business; by 1978-79 it met each Tuesday in the Downing University Center. In 1975-76 ASG printed its own roll of 49 members in a supplement it paid for in the Herald. The roll gave five kinds of seat: eleven representatives-at-large; thirteen student members of the university's Academic Council; college representatives, two each from Applied Arts, the College of Education, Ogden and Potter and one each from the Community College, the College of Business and Public Affairs and the Graduate College; nine class officers; and the five executive officers, who sat as members. At-large and college seats were filled in an April election. In April 1976 the only contested college race was in business and public affairs; the Herald reported that all other college candidates were unopposed or the slots were not filed for, and that unopposed candidates would take office automatically.

Congress worked by bill and resolution. ASG's own list for 1975-76 numbered its bills 1 to 13 and noted that all passed unless marked otherwise; number 10 passed amended and number 13, a student evaluation of the administration, was tabled. The same supplement listed twenty-three resolutions and printed a result for each. Resolutions asked the university for things. Several bills governed ASG's own machinery: Bill 5 defined the structure of the committee system, Bill 7 defined how the treasurer could distribute funds, Bill 11 required a detailed monthly statement of expenditure published in the Herald, and Bill 12 created a temporary summer budget with final approval due by the third meeting of the fall semester. The money was not ASG's own. The treasurer worked with the other executive officers to formulate a budget, the Office of Student Affairs approved it, and student government received its funds from that office.

Discipline ran two ways. The secretary said in 1975-76 that a member was warned after four absences and removed after two more. As the Herald described the constitution in December 1978, a member was warned of possible dismissal after four absences and could be voted out of Congress after six, with the executive committee able to excuse absences for extenuating circumstances and the question going to Congress for a vote if it did not accept them. Removal by impeachment required the attendance of three-fourths of the full membership. Six congressmen moved on 16 March 1976 to investigate Gerard Faulk, chairman of the Judicial Council, over his handling of two cases sent to that council, one of them a misuse-of-funds charge brought against the treasurer. Congress acquitted him three weeks later: 28 members were present, no votes were cast in favour of removal, twelve were cast against and sixteen abstained, and for the second straight week the three-fourths attendance was missing. In the underlying case the Judicial Council had ruled in the treasurer's favour.

Constitutional revision ran the length of the block. A bill dated 7-14 September 1976 would have created a committee to revise the constitution; the record does not say whether it passed. Changes were still awaiting approval as the fall term opened in September 1977. Congress approved a revised constitution on 30 November 1978 and passed a constitutional amendment on 8 February 1979, and the Herald reported that students ratified the revision at the April 1979 general election, 1,343 to 170. The new constitution made 24 separate races for the 24 representative-at-large seats, which the administrative vice president said was meant to force competition between candidates, and changed the duties of the activities vice president to line up with the new University Center Board. By 1979-80 a constitutional amendment guaranteed eight on-campus, eight off-campus and eight general-representative seats on the congress in place of representation by 24 representatives at-large. Legislation surviving from that session carries year-prefixed numbering, as with Resolution 79-10 of 13 November 1979 on staffing for academic scholarships.

Funding moved the other way. After ASG lost more than $23,000 on major concerts, the dean of student affairs recommended halving its entertainment budget; on 29 April 1978 the regents let ASG keep its full $62,000 entertainment budget for 1978-79 and directed that a committee study who should control programming. On 31 March 1979 the regents approved a rebuilt University Center Board and funded it with $80,000 for programming, taking over from the autumn the lectures and concerts ASG had run. ASG's president, its activities vice president and one member of congress sat on the new board's committees.

The legislature

1980-81 through 1985-86

The legislative branch was the Student Congress. The constitution in force by 1985-86 vested in it all legislative authority of the Associated Student Government, the power to hear and approve the budget with recommendation for final approval by the university administration, to establish committees and agencies, to approve constitutional appointments by simple majority, and to function as a body in impeachment proceedings. Congress did not elect its own officers: ASG's five executive officers served as the officers of the Student Congress. The president presided over all regular and special meetings and was a non-voting member except to resolve a vote; the administrative vice-president, public relations vice-president, secretary and treasurer voted.

Membership was the president and vice-president of each of the four classes, one Academic Council representative and one alternate from each academic college, five on-campus, five off-campus and five at-large representatives, two freshman representatives elected each autumn, and the five executive officers. All but the freshman officers and representatives were elected in the spring, on dates the Rules and Elections Committee set at the new Congress's first meeting subject to Congress's approval. Representatives needed a 2.25 academic standing and, except for freshman class officers and representatives, one prior semester at Western; Academic Council representatives needed 2.75. Terms ran one academic year and began at least two weeks before the previous one ended. The amendment sheet bound with that constitution shows the design still moving: four freshman representatives instead of two, a delegate from every recognised student organisation, and vacancies filled after a two-week application period. What the corresponding documents said in 1980-81 has not been established here.

Congress met on Tuesdays, though not always; the meeting of 29 April 1982 fell on a Thursday. Legislation took two readings, normally a week apart, unless the rules were suspended, as they were on 6 May 1980, when a motion to suspend for Resolution 80-1 was defeated 20-8 with one abstention and a later suspension carried, and again on 9 April 1985. The Legislative Research Committee collected and reviewed every bill after its first reading, determined its constitutionality and researched conflicts with earlier legislation. Four recorded divisions survive from 1985-86, 26-4, 16-14, 4-28 and 7-14; the rest of the surviving measures passed unanimously or on a voice vote.

Seats went unfilled and members went absent. Under the constitution vacancies were filled by presidential appointment with the consent of a majority of Congress, and on 29 April 1982 Congress approved appointments to on-campus, off-campus, at-large and college seats in a single sitting, the new members sworn in by Judicial Council chairperson Gail Williams. At that meeting Treasurer Kelly Cook moved two by-law amendments on absence: one required a member absent from more than three meetings to appear before Congress and show cause why the seat should not be removed, after which Congress voted on removal; the other made one unexcused absence from a committee meeting equivalent to one unexcused absence from a Congress meeting. A bill requiring college representatives who missed two Academic Council meetings to come before the Judicial Council for possible dismissal was introduced on 8 April 1986 and passed unanimously on its second reading, 15 April 1986. A bill adopting a new application for open seats failed 7-14 on 1 April 1986, and a bill letting absent authors and sponsors vote by written approval filed with the recording secretary failed 4-28 after its first reading of 25 February 1986.

Congress legislated on residence-hall inspection, grading and withdrawal rules, campaign and election regulations, a book exchange and a student discount card, state education funding, and on its own machinery: abolishing the Finance Committee, creating a Financial Advisory Council, writing councils into the by-laws, opening college voting to every student's majors and minors, moving the Graduate College election to the autumn, and making the Kentucky Intercollegiate State Legislature a standing committee. Resolutions ran to 80-34 in 1980-81 and 81-13 in 1981-82, of which the archive holds 24 and all 13. In 1985-86 bills and resolutions shared one numbering sequence per semester, 17 numbers in the autumn and 39 in the spring, every one of which survives except 86-14-S.

The legislature

1986-87 through 1991-92

The legislative body of the Associated Students was its Congress. Its regular meeting was on Tuesday, and where the SGA records give a start time it is 5 p.m. For 1986-87 through spring 1992 the collection holds 135 sets of minutes and agendas. Of these, 127 are the Bowling Green body rather than the Glasgow campus, and 125 of those carry a Tuesday date; the two exceptions are 28 November 1988 and 27 October 1990. Survival is uneven by session: 25 sets for 1986-87, 6 for 1987-88, 24 for 1988-89, 36 for 1989-90 (28 of them Bowling Green), 28 for 1990-91 and 16 for 1991-92. All six surviving 1987-88 sets record a 5 p.m. start, and 68 sets across the block do.

Congress worked through bills and resolutions, numbered in separate series that restarted each semester and carried an F or S suffix. The two series could carry the same number in one term: spring 1991 produced both Bill #91-7-S, on presidential scholar recognition, and Resolution 91-7-S, on an intramural field. The surviving originals held in this repository were printed on Associated Student Government letterhead with a status block for first reading, second reading, pass, fail and other, a purpose line, whereas clauses and a therefore clause. Author and sponsor lines appear but not on every measure and not always both. On Resolution 91-2-F a standing committee, Student Affairs, occupies the author line and there is no sponsor line; on Resolution 91-6-F a named member is the sponsor and there is no author line. The legislature

1992-93 through 1998-99

Through these seven years the legislative branch was called Congress. Its own minutes use the term from 1993 on, and in September 2000 SGA's website still described Congress as the legislative body of SGA, charged with creating legislation. The rename from Associated Student Government to Student Government Association was proposed in Bill 92-10-S, a constitutional amendment dated 1 April 1992; the archive does not record how or when it took effect. The minutes that survive from October 1993, March 1994, February 1995 and September 1996 all record a 5 p.m. start on a Tuesday, and by September 2000 Congress met every Tuesday at 5 p.m. in Downing University Center 305. Each surviving set of minutes opens with a roll call, and names the absent.

Numbered legislation was the instrument. Every measure carried a code: two-digit calendar year, sequence number, and F or S for the semester, as Bill 93-1-F or Resolution 98-7-F. Bills and resolutions ran in separate sequences and both restarted each semester, so Bill 93-1-F, a set of constitutional amendments, and Resolution 93-1-F, on trash cans at shuttle stops, are different measures from the same session. A measure was introduced at a first reading and voted at a second, usually a week or two later; the printed cover sheet carried boxes for first reading, second reading, pass, fail and other, with the dates written in and the outcome marked. Congress could suspend its bylaws to take a measure in one sitting, as it did for Resolution 94-5-S on 29 March 1994. Bills allocated money, created programmes and amended the constitution and bylaws. Resolutions asked the university, its department heads or its Board of Regents for something.

Bill 93-1-F, Amendments to the Present Constitution, introduced 7 September 1993, proposed to rewrite who sat there. It provided twenty-four representatives at large drawn from off-campus and commuter students, eight each from the sophomore, junior and senior classes; five non-traditional representatives; five Academic Council representatives from each existing college, with an alternate for each college appointed by the president; residence hall representatives apportioned by hall population, one for a hall under 200 students rising to four for a hall of 600 or more; and a Freshman Council of eight. Everyone but the hall representatives and the Freshman Council was to be elected with the executive officers in the campus-wide election, on dates set by the Executive Council; those two groups were to be elected by the second Tuesday in September. Under the same document the vice president presided over Congress, could call a special meeting on twenty-four hours' notice, and voted only to break a tie. A revised constitution was in force by the following spring, when Bill 94-5-S of 29 March 1994 amended the bylaws to fit it, and by February 1995 vice president Tara Higdon was calling meetings to order.

Seats went unfilled, and the paper structure was never the real one. SGA's membership list for the autumn of 1994 names five executive officers and twenty-nine other members: six senior, eight junior and eight sophomore off-campus representatives, four college representatives, from Potter, Graduate, Ogden and Education, and three non-traditional representatives. It lists no residence hall representatives and no Freshman Council. On 28 February 1995 the secretary read twenty open positions into the minutes, nine of them elected seats and eleven of them single seats in named residence halls, and Congress voted three people into seats that night. The legislature

1999-2000 through 2003-04

The legislative branch was called the Student Congress. SGA's own site said in September 2000 that Congress met every Tuesday at 5 p.m. in Downing University Center room 305, and its legislative branch page in January 2004 called Congress the one branch a student could join by filling out an application rather than by election or appointment, with an election held if applicants outnumbered seats. A member had to be enrolled full or part time, be in good standing and hold a 2.25 grade point average, and had to serve on at least one committee; the application form warned that after three absences the Judicial Council could terminate the seat.

The constitution as amended in spring 2001 gave Congress five non-traditional and five graduate representatives, ten representatives from each of the four classes, residence hall seats apportioned from one to four by hall population, ten at-large seats, an Academic Council representative and an alternate for each college, and the Coordinator of Committees and every standing and ad hoc committee chair as presidential appointments. Amendment 3 replaced that clause with two graduate representatives, two from each class, the same hall apportionment, ten at-large places, five off-campus places and two from each academic college, with the same appointed positions; academic college representatives needed a declared major, a 2.75 overall grade point average and a 3.0 in the major. The date Amendment 3 was ratified is not established, and SGA's site was still posting the Spring 2001 text with six appended amendments in January 2004. Sophomore, junior and senior class seats were filled in spring elections and the remaining elected seats in the fall; members took office by the second week of September and served the rest of the academic year. Congress approved the budget for the university's final approval, sat as a judicial body in impeachments, could override a veto by a two-thirds vote, and amended the constitution by a two-thirds vote of its full membership followed by ratification in a student referendum, with the Judicial Council reviewing amendments first.

The constitutional committees were the Committee on Committees, which only Congress members could join, and Legislative Research, Student Affairs, Academic Affairs, Public Relations and Campus Improvements, all open to any student, each with a chair appointed by the president and approved by a majority of Congress. SGA's own site also listed Hillraisers and Special Events among its committees in 2000, a Senior Banquet committee in 2001, and an ad hoc Organizational Aid Committee in 2003-04. Legislation was numbered by semester and marked bill or resolution, was sponsored by committees, and went to the Legislative Research Committee before its second reading. SGA's legislation archive holds nothing for 1999, 2000, 2001 or spring 2002; it lists twenty pieces for fall 2002, thirteen for spring 2003, twenty for fall 2003 and twelve for spring 2004. The Herald reported on 29 April 2003 that the year had closed after more than 55 pieces of legislation.

Attendance was a running problem. The Herald of 20 April 2000 carried an item headlined for still-declining SGA membership. At the fourteenth meeting of the year, on 2 December 2003, 25 of 43 members were present.

Bill 03-10-F, a new constitution and bylaws written by Legislative Research Committee chair Robert Watkins, was entered in SGA's legislation archive on 10 November 2003, and the minutes record that it passed with general consent on 2 December 2003. It renamed Congress the Senate and set 35 seats: four from each of the four classes, two graduate representatives and seventeen at-large. It created a Speaker of the Senate, elected from the elected Senate by the previous semester's legislative branch, who presided, served as Coordinator of Committees and appointed a Secretary of the Senate subject to Senate approval. It lowered the seat requirement to a 2.0 grade point average and full-time enrolment, raised the veto override to three-fourths, and left vacancies after the fall elections to presidential appointment with majority Senate approval. The accompanying bylaws set a simple-majority quorum, allowed four absences a semester unless excused, required committee service of every senator but the Speaker and the University Senate representatives, and were to take effect for fall 2004.

The Herald reported that the vice presidency of finance was folded into administration, that public relations became an appointed post, that the branch would shrink from 75 to 35 and be elected by students where empty seats had previously been filled by appointment, and that the executive branch could no longer present legislation to the senate. On 22 January 2004 it reported that no vote had yet been taken because two-thirds of the body had not been present at the previous two meetings, and that President John Bradley said all 75 positions had never been filled during his time in SGA. On 16 March it reported that approval by the legislative branch had taken five attempts for want of attendance, and that students ratified the constitution in a referendum on 9 March 2004. The election codes adopted on 22 January 2004 set internet polling from 16 to 17 March 2004, and the Herald of 18 March reported that 1,232 students voted and that the first senate under the new constitution was chosen at the same time. On 15 April 2004 the Herald reported that Watkins had been elected the first speaker of the senate by 9 votes to 8 over Brittany Fausey, 17 of the 20 members present voting, and that the result was questioned at once because the new constitution was unclear whether two-thirds of eligible voters or of total membership was required and because three members present had been barred from voting under a judicial council ruling. Fausey did not challenge the result.

The legislature

2004-05 through 2009-10

The constitution proposed in the autumn of 2003 and put to students the following spring named the legislative branch the Student Senate. The body it replaced had been called Congress, and the new text still slipped into the old name twice, at 3.4.4 and 3.10.4. The Senate had thirty-five seats: four senators from each of the four class years, two graduate senators and seventeen at large. Its list of seats was exhaustive, and it reserved none for a residence hall, an academic college or off-campus students, all of which the constitution it replaced had provided for. Twenty-eight seats were filled at the spring election and seven, the four freshman seats and three at large, in the autumn, and graduate seats left empty in the spring became at-large seats in the autumn. Senators elected in the spring took office no sooner than two weeks after the fall term began, those elected in the autumn no sooner than two weeks after that election, and both served one academic year. A Senate seat required a 2.0 cumulative grade point average, good standing and full-time enrolment. The amendments proposed in the spring of 2005 raised that to 2.5.

The Senate elected a Speaker from among its own elected members, by a vote of the previous semester's legislative branch. The Speaker presided, served as Coordinator of Committees, appointed the Secretary of the Senate subject to the Senate's approval, called special meetings on twenty-four hours' notice, and reported excessive absences to the Judicial Council. Five standing committees, Legislative Research, Student Affairs, Academic Affairs, Public Relations and Campus Improvements, were written into the constitution as part of the legislative branch and were open to any student who wished to serve. The 2004 constitution put their chairs in the Speaker's gift. The 2005 amendments moved that appointment to the President, and added a vote of no confidence in the Speaker and a procedure for removing a committee head, neither of which the 2004 text had. The bylaws of the same season did not follow: they still gave the chairs to the Speaker, and the two governing documents remained in conflict on the point.

Under the 2004 bylaws the Senate met at 5 p.m. every Tuesday of the academic year in the Senate Chambers unless a majority agreed otherwise, and a simple majority of the membership was a quorum. By November 2009 SGA's website was giving the room as DUC 305. Legislation needed a committee sponsor, took two readings, and went to the Legislative Research Committee in between with an author present. Once passed, the Executive Cabinet had fourteen days to ratify or reject it, and silence counted as approval. The two documents disagreed on what it took to override a veto: the constitution said three-fourths of the Senate, the bylaws two-thirds of those present and voting, and the bylaws cited the constitution for the figure they contradicted. The 2005 amendments brought the constitution down to two-thirds. The Senate also approved the budget, confirmed constitutional appointments, wrote its own bylaws and election code, and sat as the trial body in impeachments, which took the written endorsement of a fifth of the membership to begin, a special session under the Chief Justice, at least fourteen days before a final vote, and two-thirds of the full membership to carry.

Elections were run by the Judicial Council under codes it enacted subject to a majority vote of the Senate. The codes adopted in January 2004 capped spending by a candidate for the legislature at $50, against $450 for a candidate for president, and set internet polling for 16 and 17 March 2004.

Filling the chamber was another matter. The autumn of 2009 is the stretch the archive documents best: the roll ran 23 on 8 September, 24 on 15 September and 32 on 22 September, once the judicial council had certified the fall election. On 29 September the minutes put it at 36, one seat more than the constitution allowed. It stood at 32 through October, then at 30 on 27 October, after three senators resigned on the floor on 20 October and a fourth on 27 October. SGA's website that November listed twenty-nine people on the legislative side: twenty-seven student senators, the Speaker and the Secretary of the Senate.

SGA numbered its own bodies in its minutes. The senate that opened on 5 September 2006 called itself the first meeting of the fifth Student Government Association, and was still the fifth senate that December. The 2009-10 senate called itself the eighth Senate in every set of its minutes examined here, from September 2009 through April 2010. Its legislation numbering had reversed along the way: spring 2004 bills ran year first, as in B 04-1-S, while by the autumn of 2006 the sequence came first, as in Bill 11-06-F, and it still did in Bill 30-10-S in April 2010. No document found dates the change.

The legislature

2017-18 through 2026-27

The legislative branch was the Student Senate. Its constitution made it the official voice of the student body, gave it the power to establish bylaws, had it approve the budget with recommendation for final approval by the university administration, and let it override an Executive Cabinet veto by a two-thirds vote. Under the bylaws it met at 5 p.m. every Tuesday of the academic year in the Senate Chambers unless it agreed otherwise. Sittings were numbered by session and by meeting: the sixteenth Senate sat in 2017-18, the twenty-fourth in 2024-25, and the twenty-fifth in 2025-26, whose twenty-sixth meeting fell on 14 April 2026.

The constitution in force for 2017-18 listed 36 seats: three each for the senior, junior, sophomore and freshman classes, two graduate, one English as a Second Language International, one Gatton Academy, one from each of six academic colleges, and fourteen at large. Bill 12-21-S, first read 2 March 2021, moved the academic-college senators, the international senator and three at-large seats from the fall election to the spring, leaving three freshman, one graduate and one Gatton Academy seat to the fall, with any unfilled fall seat becoming an at-large seat in the same election. By 2023 the constitution counted five academic-college senators, and Bill 13-23-F, read on 14 and 28 November 2023, took that back to six by adding a Mahurin Honors College senator. The constitution now posted lists 38 seats: one graduate senator rather than two, the international senator returned to the fall election, and new seats for transfer and non-traditional students, the Intercultural Student Engagement Center and first-generation students.

Committees were rebuilt mid-decade. The 2017-18 constitution named eight: Legislative Research, Student Affairs, Academic Affairs, Public Relations, Campus Improvements, Diversity and Inclusion, Sustainability and SAVES, their heads appointed by the President with the approval of a majority of the Senate. Most of those names, together with an Organizational Aid Committee, were still sponsoring legislation in spring 2021. From the fall of 2021 the sponsors named in the archive are Legislative Operations, Enrollment and Student Experience, Outreach and Student Experience, Campus Improvements, Diversity Equity and Inclusion, and Student Mental Health and Wellbeing. Bill 21-22-S, read on 8 and 22 March 2022, proposed a Community Relations Committee to advocate for students' needs in Bowling Green and encourage civic involvement rather than a political agenda; the archive records the bill as tabled, and the committee first appears as a sponsor in October 2022. Bill 4-22-F, passed 36-1 on 11 October 2022, renamed three of the committees. Bill 32-24-S, first read 12 March 2024, moved the appointment of committee heads from the President to the Speaker, still subject to a two-thirds vote of the Senate. The constitution now posted lists seven standing committees, ending with Action & Opportunity and Community Relations, and by 2025-26 the archive also shows a Campus Safety and Security Committee and a Bylaws Review Committee sponsoring legislation.

SGA is given $100,000 each school year. The Executive Cabinet drafts the budget, the Senate approves it, and the university administration gives final approval. The 2024-25 budget included $20,000 for organizational aid, $23,500 for scholarships and $10,705 in legislative discretionary funding, and passed at the first meeting of the 24th Senate on 27 August 2024. The bylaws require that the Legislative Discretionary Fund never fall below a tenth of the total budget as proposed by the Chief Financial Officer. At the twenty-sixth meeting of the 25th Senate, on 14 April 2026, the Chief Financial Officer reported $71.17 remaining, and the last two bills of the night were amended to spend it.

The rules for membership tightened in the same decade. Bill 29-24-S raised the minimum cumulative grade point average to 2.75 for executive officers and 2.5 for senators; Bill 26-24-S directed the removal of any member censured twice in one academic year; Bill 22-24-S changed the threshold for a constitutional amendment from two-thirds of the full Senate membership to two-thirds of those present; Bill 23-24-S moved amendment referendums into the fall and spring senator elections. The bylaws adopted 24 February 2026 allowed no more than two unexcused Senate absences a semester before the Speaker referred a member to the Judicial Council.

The recorded disputes turned on money, on Greek life, and on who controlled the Senate's own business. In August 2017 the Senate rejected Bill 5-17-F, which would have barred the executive branch from writing legislation, by 4-8, and in November 2017 Bill 32-17-F, which would have removed the first reading, failed. In October 2018 Bill 10-18-F, which would have barred SGA funding to Interfraternity Council and Panhellenic Council chapters, was defeated 16-17. In March 2024 Bill 32-24-S took the appointment of committee heads from the President and gave it to the Speaker.

Legislation was numbered N-YY-F in the fall and N-YY-S in the spring. The spring series began again at one in most sessions, though in 2022-23 it continued the fall numbering. SGA publishes the record on two web pages, one covering Fall 2011 to Spring 2025 and one covering Fall 2025 to Spring 2026, each session in its own table giving the item's title linked to its file, its number, authors, sponsoring committee, both reading dates and whether the Senate and the Executive Cabinet passed it, with some of those fields left blank. The tables list 71 items for 2017-18, 55 for 2018-19, 16 for 2019-20, 30 for 2020-21, 56 for 2021-22, 54 for 2022-23, 54 for 2023-24, 48 for 2024-25 and 60 for 2025-26.

The judiciary

1980-81 through 1991-92

The third branch of the Associated Student Government in these years was the Judicial Council. The fullest account of it the archive holds is a typescript constitution TopSCHOLAR dates to 1986, "Constitution of WKU Associated Students with Amendments", bound with an undated sheet of amendments. What follows describes that document. How far its terms reach across this period is not established here: Paul J. Deom's letter of February 1982 cites the judicial provisions as article IX, so a differently numbered text governed in 1980-82, and twenty-one amendments students approved on 11 November 1987 took effect only after the spring 1988 elections.

In that text, Article I, Section 2 divided ASG into an executive branch, a legislative branch, a judicial branch and committees established by the president and by Congress, and Article IV, Section 1 vested the judicial authority in the Judicial Council. The council had seven regular members and three alternates, appointed by the ASG president with the approval of a two-thirds vote of the full membership of Congress and taking office no later than two weeks after the fall term began, though Article II, Section 3 of the same document says judicial appointments needed only a simple majority. The president named the chairman from among the seven; the chairman presided, set the time and place of meetings, and administered the oath of office to ASG's officers and to Congress. The council elected one alternate as clerk, its records were kept in the ASG office, and alternates were designated first, second and third and filled vacancies in that order. A member had to have attended Western the semester immediately preceding, remain a student and not a faculty or staff member for the whole term, stay off disciplinary probation, hold an academic standing of 2.25 or higher, sit outside Congress, and not have been a candidate for an ASG executive office in the year of appointment. The base text appointed three persons each academic year to a one-year term; the amendment sheet raised that to seven, and rewrote two of the qualifications.

Five duties were listed: to interpret and clarify the constitution and by-laws at the request of the Executive Council, the president or Congress; to hear appeals from inferior courts; to exercise judicial review over acts of Congress at the request of a Congress member, the president, the Executive Council or any registered student; to hear all appeals from the Rules and Elections Committee over election disputes, including qualifications for candidacy; and to set its own procedural rules and ethical considerations. A council member or alternate could be removed for just cause by a three-quarters vote of the full membership of Congress acting as a judicial body, on the written endorsement of five members of Congress or five of the council, with no final vote until fourteen days had passed; the amendment sheet lowered that to two-thirds. Impeachment of executive officers and of Congress members was Congress's own work, by a two-thirds vote of its full membership on the written endorsement of five congressional members, again after fourteen days. The Rules and Elections Committee decided election disputes subject only to appeal to the council.

What the archive records of the council at work is intermittent. Deom wrote in February 1982 that Steve Fuller had appointed him in autumn 1980 on the understanding of a two-year term, that the executive council and the president had decided to replace him, and that the report of a new council being named each January was wrong. Marcel Bush called the council "virtually non-functioning", said it had met once to her knowledge in her three years in ASG, said two of the seven seats were active when she became president, and said she told David Payne after her resignation to appoint a new council as soon as possible. Six of the seven appointees were sworn in at the ASG meeting of Tuesday 26 January 1982 - chairman Gale Williams, Bob Dillard, Regenia Giles, Rick Murphy, Dave Hoffmann and Jill Capps - with Archie Beck absent and to be sworn in later; three alternates were also appointed. Margaret Ragan, administrative vice president, said the council was made up of students who had never served on ASG or run for an executive office, and the report added that members served one year and that the council would hold one scheduled meeting a year but could meet whenever a controversy arose.

In March 1987 Greg Robertson, vice chairman of the Rules and Elections Committee, described the council as ASG's judicial authority and as the body a disqualified candidate was appealing to; in that election 38 candidates filed for 41 seats and 25 were unopposed, against 20 of 44 unopposed the year before. A roster survives for 1986-87: seven regular members, Kent Groemling, Bill Schilling, Naheed Shafi, Jerry Castleberry, Dwight Austion, Jennifer Borsch and Dan Wooten as chair, and three alternates, Stacey Crain, Keith Wallace and Mary Kate Erwin. Amos Gott, ASG president in 1989-90, chaired the council in 1990-91 and resigned in January 1991 after a new part-time job as adviser for fraternity affairs made him ineligible; the Herald reported that the ASG constitution bars faculty and staff members from serving.

The judiciary

1992-93 through 2003-04

The judicial branch of WKU student government was the Judicial Council. The constitution as amended in spring 2001 vested the association's judicial authority in it and named it one of three branches alongside Congress and the executive. The 2000 Election Codes, citing Article IV, Section 5, opened by stating that the council supervised all elections.

Under the 2001 constitution the council had five justices. The president appointed them and Congress confirmed them, by a majority vote under the amendment package Bill 93-1-F, introduced on 7 September 1993, and by a two-thirds vote under the 2001 text. The president named one of the five as chief justice, subject to a majority vote of Congress. Justices served one academic year and took office no later than two weeks after the fall term began. They had to have been enrolled at Western for the year before appointment, hold a 2.5 cumulative grade point average, stay enrolled and in good standing, and not sit in Congress at the same time. Vacancies were filled within two weeks, and the council's records were posted for two weeks before being filed in the SGA office. The 2001 constitution had the chief justice schedule bi-weekly meetings; the bylaws proposed in spring 2002 required the council to meet at least twice a month in the SGA offices, and to comply with the Kentucky Open Meetings Law.

Its powers were listed. It interpreted the constitution and bylaws on request. It held judicial review over the acts of Congress, at the request of any registered student. It heard all appeals about elections and candidates' qualifications. It wrote the election rules, subject to a majority vote of Congress. It reviewed constitutional amendments before Congress voted on them. It could exempt an executive candidate from the requirement of a year's prior membership in Congress, a power that Amendment 4 later removed when it fixed service requirements for each office and barred exemptions. The chief justice swore in the officers and members of Congress and presided over impeachments of executive and legislative officers; a justice's own impeachment was presided over by a member of Congress instead. Under the spring 2002 bylaws the council also handled attendance: Congress members were allowed three absences a semester, with no distinction between excused and unexcused, and a member with four or more was referred to the council, had to appear and explain, and had an answer within seven days. A justice with two unexcused absences was subject to impeachment.

The election codes dated 7 March 2000 set out the routine: a compulsory candidates' orientation meeting fourteen days before the primary, a random draw for ballot position, polling places in Margie Helm Library, the Preston Center and Downing University Center from 9 a.m. to 5 p.m., itemised campaign finance reports filed with the council, and the count, which the chief justice opened by unlocking the ballot boxes. Spending was capped at $750 for a president and vice president ticket, $400 for other executive candidates and $50 for a Congress seat, with no donor giving more than $100. Ballots were kept 72 hours and a recount had to be requested in writing within 48. If a majority of the council found an impropriety it held a new election, contested only by candidates already eligible. The codes adopted on 22 January 2004 reset the caps at $450 for president, $400 for executive vice president, $350 for the other cabinet offices and $50 for legislative seats, shortened appeals to three business days, and moved voting to internet polling on 16 and 17 March 2004.

Three rosters survive. In 2000-01 Nathan Hopper was chief justice with Adrienne Frey, Heidi Grogg, Joe Morell and Brandon Copeland; SGA's website described the five as reviewing legislation when Congress or the Executive Cabinet asked, overseeing the homecoming queen and executive elections, and interpreting the constitution on request. In 2001-02 Heidi Grogg was chief justice with Copeland, Robert Unseld Jr., Amanda Demarest and Mike Richardson. Holly Skidmore was chief justice in 2002-03 and made the spring 2003 general election results public shortly after 1 a.m. on the Friday, the poll having closed the previous night. By January 2004 the council was Chief Justice Troy Ransdell with Scott Broadbent, Josh Collins, Gretchen Light and Kristin Hartley, and the website told applicants that appointments needed the approval of Congress and that prior service in Congress was expected.

The Herald index for April 1999 lists an item on the Judicial Council questioning candidate Joe Matheis in the vice president of finance race, and another on 27 April on Ryan Morrison winning an SGA election. Those articles have not been read for this account, so no result, margin or ruling is stated here. The index also lists a report headed on confirmed election violations in the issue of 23 April 2002, likewise not read. Nothing else about the council between 1992-93 and 1999-2000 has been located in the sources consulted here.

The council's own code of conduct, dated 8 September 2003, set the order of a hearing: five minutes for the opening argument by the person requesting an interpretation, five for each rebuttal, then deliberation, then a verdict that did not have to be given at that meeting but had to appear in the minutes. It recorded a separation of powers rule under which no justice served on a congressional committee or authored legislation, and reserved the council's right to refuse a request for interpretation it deemed frivolous or unwarranted.

SGA's minutes archive listed six Judicial Council meetings for 2003-04, running from September to December 2003. Two of the six files survive. The first, of 8 September 2003, recorded four justices with the fifth seat still unfilled, the adoption of a code of conduct, and a tentative weekly meeting time. The second, of 6 October 2003, recorded attendance hearings for three Congress members who did not appear to defend themselves and a decision to remove them. SGA's index page dates that meeting 10 October, while the file and its own heading give 6 October.

The constitution proposed in fall 2003 kept the Judicial Council at five justices appointed by the president with a two-thirds vote of what was now the Senate, and extended judicial review from the acts of Congress alone to the actions of both the Executive Cabinet and the Senate. It required the council to meet no less than twice a month, barred justices from membership in any other branch, and gave the council discretion over the timeline, applications and campaigning for the special election that KRS 164.321 required when the student regent seat fell vacant. Impeachment in every case needed the written endorsement of a fifth of the Senate, a wait of fourteen days, and a two-thirds vote of the full Senate sitting as a judicial body, with the chief justice presiding except when a justice was in the dock, when the Speaker did. The bylaws adopted with it added a power of censure: a request from ten or more people against any executive or legislative officer was investigated by the council, which issued a censure if it found the officer neglecting or wanting in their duties, while a request against a justice went to the other four with the president presiding and breaking ties. Senators were allowed four absences a semester unless the council excused them.

The council's reach was written into the constitution itself in spring 2002, when voters passed the Supremacy Clause amendment, making the constitution the supreme law of the association and putting every ruling and enactment under it, subject to federal, state and university law, and making it the duty of Congress members, executive officers and justices alike to know its contents.

The judiciary

2004-05 through 2013-14

SGA's judicial branch in these years was the Judicial Council. The constitution vested the association's judicial authority in it, and the council had five justices throughout the period. The president appointed them and the legislative body confirmed each by a two-thirds vote; the president also named the chief justice from among the five, subject to a majority vote. Justices took office no later than two weeks after the fall term began and served one academic year, and vacancies were to be filled within two weeks of a resignation. A justice needed a 2.5 cumulative grade point average. The council picked one justice as clerk at its first meeting.

The rulebook did not stay still. Under the constitution as amended spring 2001, still posted on SGA's site in November 2004, judicial review ran over the acts of Congress, a justice reported to Congress once a month, and a justice was barred from sitting in Congress. The text proposed in spring 2005 extended judicial review to the Executive Cabinet and the Senate at the request of any registered student not on the council, broadened the bar to any other branch of SGA, set the council to meet no less than twice a month, and added a requirement that a justice have completed a year as an elected or appointed SGA officer unless the Senate granted an exemption. By the version posted in October 2012 that qualification had gone, the chief justice had instead to have served a year as an associate justice, and the reporting duty had become twice a month. No source found here dates those changes.

The council's other written powers were steady. It interpreted the constitution and bylaws on request, heard election appeals and questions of candidacy, enacted and reviewed the election codes subject to a majority vote of the legislative body, and reviewed every constitutional amendment before that body voted on it. It acted under the attendance policy: senators were allowed no more than three absences a semester unless the council excused them, and the Speaker referred a senator with excessive absences, or one already censured, to the council, which had to meet and decide within seven days. A request signed by no fewer than ten students obliged the council to investigate an executive or legislative officer for censure, censure being defined in the bylaws as an act of the council expressing disapproval of an officer's action; a request against a justice went to the other four, with the president presiding and breaking ties. The chief justice presided over impeachments of executive and legislative officers and administered the oath of office. A justice could be removed only by the Senate, on the written endorsement of a fifth of its membership and a two-thirds vote of its full membership, with the Speaker in the chair.

The election codes adopted on 22 January 2004 made the council the supervisor of all SGA elections. They required a candidate orientation meeting, with disqualification for a candidate who failed to attend unless the council granted an exemption; set spending limits from $450 for president down to $50 for legislative candidates; required itemised expenditure reports filed with the council; gave three business days after the results for appeals, protests and disqualifications; and required a new election if a majority of the council found an impropriety. The council met once a week during a campaign and reported its rulings to the legislature.

No Judicial Council minutes survive for this decade, and what is known of the council's work comes from SGA's senate minutes, its own posted opinions and the Herald. In February 2009, after a resignation left the student regent seat empty, the council applied the clause that gave the executive vice president the presidency but not the Board of Regents seat and required a special election for the seat under KRS 164.321, with the council holding discretion over the timeline, applications and campaigning. The Herald of 12 February 2009 reported that the council voted 2-1 on 10 February to hold that election, that Chief Justice Lisa Kappler cast the dissent, and that she resigned two days later in a letter saying she wanted to spend more time on school and family. The Herald of 10 November 2009 reported the council removing one senator for unexcused absences and reviewing a second who was allowed to remain. The Herald of 15 November 2011 reported that ten of the senate's 36 members had been called before the council over the fall semester and that two were dismissed after failing to attend their hearings. In April 2013 the council disqualified the president-elect 3-2 over an election-code violation, then met again and decided unanimously not to contest the administrator's memorandum reinstating her, saying it did not believe it had the power to do so while recording its disapproval.

Two further rulings fell in 2013. On 29 August the council ruled unconstitutional a proposed amendment adding an international student senate seat, before the Senate voted on it; its authors, the president and the executive vice president, reworded it and the Senate passed the revision the following month. On 21 November the council ruled unconstitutional a senator's motion reversing SGA's support for a Talisman student fee, and ruled the 2005 policy behind that support unconstitutional as well.

The written office changed at the end of the period. Amendments the Senate adopted on 26 February 2013 and referred to the spring 2013 ballot made a justice's term the length of the justice's academic career, gave the council review over the enactments and impeachment proceedings of regional affiliates, and let it open a censure investigation on a majority of its own members rather than only on a ten-student request. A draft the council posted in 2014 would have raised it to seven justices, moved the choice of chief justice from the president to a majority of the justices serving in the spring, and created an associate chief justice. An amendment on those lines passed the Senate in February 2014 but was declared unconstitutional afterwards by the Executive Council, which found too few senators had been present for the vote to count, and the SGA website still described a five-justice council when the judicial page was last modified on 5 March 2014. All three changes appear in the constitution as amended spring 2017. Nothing found here shows when between 2014 and 2017 they took effect.

The judiciary

2014-15 through 2026-27

The judicial branch of the Student Government Association was the Judicial Council. The constitution vested SGA's judicial authority in it, set it at seven justices, and made them presidential appointees confirmed by a two-thirds vote of the Senate. A justice had to be in good standing with the university, be a student for the whole term of office, and not belong to another branch, and held the seat for the duration of an academic career while meeting the requirements of the office. The council appointed its own chief justice by majority vote, subject to a majority of the Senate, and the chief justice appointed an associate chief justice, also approved by the Senate. A chief justice had to have completed a year as an associate justice, or be granted an exemption by the Senate. That wording stood in the 2017, 2023-24 and 2024-25 texts, and the council page published in 2026 described the branch the same way.

The council's duties were to interpret the constitution and bylaws on request, to exercise judicial review of Executive Cabinet and Senate actions at the request of any registered student not on the council, to rule on the absences and office hours of senators and officers, to enact and review election codes subject to a Senate vote, to run the code meetings candidates had to attend, to hear election complaints, and to hold censure hearings. Under the General Procedural Rules the Senate adopted in spring 2022, quorum was five members minus the number of vacancies. The bill supporting their adoption recorded that the council had previously had no formal written guidelines or procedures for its functions and duties.

The branch's own record is unevenly published, and this account does not cover the whole period. No minutes, opinions or roster survive for 2014-15 or 2016-17, and the roster posted for 2015-16 carries no date. Written opinions were posted only for Fall 2012 to April 2014. Minutes are posted for 2017-18, 2019-20 and 2021-22 through 2025-26, with a single file for 2020-21 and two opinions for 2018-19.

Where the record survives, the council ruled against other parts of SGA. On 21 November 2013, in Opinion 2013FA-006, it held that Bill 08-05-S of 2005, the Talisman student referendum, had never been passed by a route the bylaws authorised, was unconstitutional when passed and had never represented SGA's position, which also made the Senate's vote the same day to rescind it null. On 3 December 2015 it voided Bills 8-15-F and 9-15-F under Bylaw 6.1, because SGA could allocate money only to students, cancelling $1,500 for the physics department and up to $759 for art. On 3 December 2021 it held that where the constitution and the bylaws conflicted over the executive veto the constitution governed, and that the bylaws had to be amended to match. On 12 November 2025, in a hearing recorded as The Executive Cabinet v. Ismail, it declined by 7-0 to censure Senator Jade Ismail.

Several chief justices left office mid-year. Kelsey Luttrell announced in December 2015 that she would step down at the end of the semester, saying the council had been publicly ridiculed and lacked support. Jacob McAndrews announced in March 2019 that he would resign to run for elected office. Erika Puhakka stepped down in March 2021, and President Garrett Edmonds left the seat for his successor. Ellen Henderson swore in Associate Chief Justice Morgan Gammons as chief justice in November 2024 before graduating, and Blake Graham stepped down in November 2025 after Sophie Stirling was elected to succeed him.

The council's duties grew by amendment, mostly on legislation written outside it. Bills 1-17-F and 2-17-F, both authored by Chief of Staff Conner Hounshell and given first reading on 28 August 2017, added a temporary committee of the three branches for formulating constitutional amendments and the updating of the constitution after student body referendums, and made the chief justice interim speaker of the Senate when the speaker was absent or removed. Bills 12-23-F and 15-23-F, authored by Chief Justice Isaac King and given first reading on 14 November 2023, raised the chief justice's office hours from one to five a week and required every member of the council to keep two office hours a month. Four further bills given first reading on 12 March 2024, written by four senators with King, Associate Chief Justice Ellen Henderson and Associate Justice Garrison Reed, made the chief justice report to the Senate weekly, required a chief justice to be eligible to serve a full academic year or resign at the end of their last full one, raised the judicial grade point floor from 2.0 to 2.5, and required removal of any member censured twice in the same academic year. The council applied that last rule on 6 November 2024, censuring Malick Ibrahim, Myricle Gholston and Karisha Petty a second time each for unexcused absences and removing all three by 7-0 votes.

By 2026 the election role was set out in detail. The spring 2026 election codes required the council to meet once a week during the election season, barred a justice from advocating for or against a candidate or giving a candidate an official interpretation of SGA policy without a council ruling, including by liking, sharing or commenting on campaign posts, and required every candidate to attend one of two code meetings on pain of disqualification unless the council granted an exemption. The bylaws codified on 24 February 2026 sent any senator with two unexcused absences in a semester to the council for an attendance hearing, and made the chief justice the presiding officer at Senate trials for committee chair removals, expulsions from the Senate, and executive or judicial impeachments.

The governing documents

1992 through 2010

The Associated Students voted on 7 April 1992 to change its name to the Student Government Association. Students approved the change in a referendum on 14 April 1992 and it took effect in fall 1992. What the governing documents said between 1992 and 2000 is not established here; the archive records amendments on the ballot in 1993, 1996 and 1999 whose text has not survived.

The oldest governing document this pass could open is a constitution headed "As amended Spring 2001 Semester," posted on SGA's website and captured in November 2004, with six numbered amendments printed after its text. Its opening clause made every enrolled student a member of SGA with the right to vote in its elections, a provision the archive elsewhere credits to the 2004 constitution but which the 2001 text already carried word for word. It set three branches: an executive branch of five officers, being the President, Executive Vice President and Vice Presidents of Finance, Public Relations and Administration; a legislative branch called Congress; and a five-member Judicial Council appointed by the president with the approval of two-thirds of Congress. The base text tied Congress seats to the four classes, to residence halls apportioned one to four by hall population, and to graduate, non-traditional and at-large members, with an Academic Council representative for each college, the Coordinator of Committees and every standing and ad hoc committee chair appointed by the president. Amendment 3 replaced that composition with a smaller one that dropped the non-traditional seats and added off-campus and academic-college representatives. Amendments needed two-thirds of the full Congress and a student referendum, and every constitutional change was subject to the university administration's approval. Amendment 6, a Supremacy Clause, is recorded on SGA's own results page as passed at the spring 2002 general election, with no vote total beside it. The bylaws posted alongside were headed "As Proposed Spring 2002 Semester"; no ratifying vote for them has been found.

Bill 03-5-S, "WWHR FM Student Referendum," is dated 25 February 2003 in SGA's legislation archive. The Herald reported that Congress voted only to put the question to students, not to impose a fee; that a yes vote would send a $3-a-semester fee for the campus radio station Revolution 91.7-WWHR to the Board of Regents for the final decision; and that executive vice president John Bradley expected the board to take it up at its 9 May 2003 quarterly meeting. Students approved the fee 1,066 to 765 in an election in which 2,014 voted, the result read out by chief justice Holly Skidmore shortly after 1 a.m. TopSCHOLAR's description of the 9 May 2003 quarterly minutes lists "library, facility improvement and radio station fees" among the agenda items, but the minutes themselves would not open, so what the board decided is not established here. A $9 Talisman yearbook fee put to students the same way in March 2005 passed 1,019 to 669, and its fate at the board is likewise unrecorded in this archive.

Bill 03-10-F, "New Constitution and Bylaws," is dated 10 November 2003 in the same archive. The constitution it carried is headed "As Proposed Fall 2003" and divides SGA into executive, legislative and judicial branches. The executive became a seven-member Executive Cabinet: President, Executive Vice President and Administrative Vice President elected, and a Chief of Staff and Directors of Public Relations, Information Technology and Academic and Student Affairs appointed, all seven voting in cabinet. The Vice Presidencies of Finance and Public Relations disappeared. Congress became the Student Senate, fixed at thirty-five seats, four from each class, two graduate and seventeen at-large, with all but the four freshman and three at-large seats elected in the spring and any seat still vacant after the fall elections filled by presidential appointment with a majority of the Senate. A Speaker of the Senate, elected from the elected Senate by the previous semester's legislative branch, served as Coordinator of Committees, appointed the Secretary of the Senate and named the committee chairs subject to Senate approval. Five standing committees sat inside the legislative branch, and a new five-member Organizational-Aid Board dispensed aid to student organisations. The Executive Cabinet kept a veto over all Senate legislation, carried by a majority of the cabinet with written reasons to the sponsoring committee within forty-eight hours; the constitution set the override at three-fourths of the Senate while the bylaws set it at two-thirds of those present and voting, and the two documents were never reconciled. The bylaws fixed quorum at a simple majority, allowed a senator four absences a semester unless excused by the Judicial Council, and set meetings at 5 p.m. every Tuesday. On a presidential vacancy the Executive Vice President took the President's duties, but the student regent seat went to a special election of the student body under KRS 164.321, where the Spring 2001 constitution had filled the whole office that way. The amendment rule stayed at two-thirds of the Senate plus a referendum, but the clause subjecting constitutional change to the university administration's approval was dropped. Election Codes adopted on 22 January 2004 gave the Judicial Council charge of all elections, set filing between 1 February and 2 March, made a candidate orientation compulsory on pain of disqualification, and capped campaign spending from $450 for president down to $50 for legislative candidates, with no donor giving more than $100.

At the March 2005 general election students adopted a list of forty amendments compiled by the Legislative Research Committee, 1,193 to 262, and all twenty-five senate candidates on the ballot were elected to twenty-eight open seats. SGA afterwards posted a constitution headed "As Proposed Spring 2005"; no source found states that this document is the forty-amendment package. Against the 2004 text it raised the grade point average for a senate seat and for senate office from 2.0 to 2.5, lowered the veto override to two-thirds, moved the appointment of committee chairs from the Speaker to the President, raised the Speaker's minimum office hours from five to eight a week, seated the Speaker on the Executive Cabinet as an ex-officio non-voting member, required the Speaker to be at least a sophomore with a year's service as a senator, and added procedures for removing a committee head and for a vote of no confidence in the Speaker. The bylaws posted with it cut permitted absences from four a semester to three, raised the student University Senate representatives from three to five, required authors to defend their legislation before the Legislative Research Committee within two weeks, and corrected the fiscal record-keeping reference to Bill 02-07-F.

After that the governing record thins to nothing. SGA's senate minutes call the 2006-07 body "the fifth Student Government Association" without saying what the first was, and its rosters stayed under the constitutional thirty-five all year: eight of eighteen senators present on 5 September 2006, twenty-two of thirty-one on 28 November and sixteen of thirty-one on 5 December. No constitutional amendment, bylaw amendment or election-code revision between 2006 and 2010 turned up in this pass, which is an absence of evidence rather than evidence that nothing changed.

The governing documents

2011 through 2026

The Student Government Association's governing documents in these years were the constitution and the Executive and Legislative Bylaws, joined from spring 2022 by the Judicial Council's General Procedural Rules, which that document ranks equal to the bylaws, and by the Election Codes the Judicial Council enacts under the constitution. The constitution set the three branches, the executive offices, the Senate's seats, the standing committees and the removal procedures, and provided that it be amended by a Senate vote and a subsequent student referendum. The archive holds no complete bylaws text before February 2026, so what the bylaws contained in the earlier years is known only through the bills that amended particular sections.

Resolution 5-13-S, written by Cory Dodds, then student body president, and sponsored by Legislative Research, had its first reading on 19 February 2013 and its second on 26 February. It adopted amendments to both the constitution and the bylaws and referred the constitutional changes to the student body for ratification on the spring 2013 ballot. The proposed changes cut each class delegation from four senators to three, gave each academic college one elected senator, cut at-large seats from seventeen to fifteen, seated the president of each regional affiliate in the Senate, extended Judicial Council terms from one year to the length of a justice's academic career, and reduced the Organizational Aid Board from ten members to six. The archive does not record the spring 2013 result. A referendum in the fall 2013 election created a Navitas and English as a Second Language international student seat: 426 of the 564 students who voted cast a ballot on it, and it passed with 310 yes votes, 73 per cent.

Committees entered the constitution one at a time. Bills 16-16-F and 17-16-F, given a second reading on 15 November 2016, made the Sustainability Committee permanent and made MyCampusToo permanent under the name the Committee for Diversity and Inclusion. Bill 27-17-S added the Committee Standing Against Violence and Ending Self-harm on 4 April 2017, passing 27-0-1. The same night Bill 21-17-S passed 24-7-0, cutting the membership grade point average floor in three clauses from 2.5 to 2.0. The Herald reported on 19 April 2017 that all six referendums on that spring's ballot passed. The text reissued as amended spring 2017 carried a 36-seat Senate, eight standing committees, and a seven-justice Judicial Council appointed by the president on a two-thirds Senate vote.

Bill 8-20-S, A Bill to Initiate Emergency Clauses, had its first reading on 14 April 2020, in response to the university's closure over COVID-19. It provided that officers and senators could extend their terms until an election could be held and that the president could extend the student regent position, and set the rescheduled spring 2020 election within three weeks of the start of the fall semester. Those provisions now stand as Section 11 of the constitution, where the deadline reads four weeks.

The text WKU posted for 2023-24 was a different document: seven standing committees under new names and thirty-seven Senate seats, among them transfer and non-traditional, First-Generation and International Student Engagement Center seats. Three bills from the Legislative Operations Committee, read on 7 and 14 November 2023, corrected the documents against themselves. Bill 8-23-F replaced University Senate with Faculty Senate, because that body had split into a Faculty Senate and a Staff Senate and SGA members were chartered only for the Faculty Senate. Bill 10-23-F brought committee names into line with names the Senate had already changed in Bill 4-22-F. Bill 9-23-F struck the bylaws sections on expelling senators, because the constitution provided for impeachment and made no mention of expulsion.

A package of constitutional amendment bills, each authored by four senators and three members of the Judicial Council and sponsored by the Legislative Operations Committee and the Judicial Council, had its second reading on 28 March 2024. Bill 20-24-S renamed the Executive Vice President and Administrative Vice President as Vice President and Chief Financial Officer, and the Director of Public Relations as Chief Communications Officer. Bill 19-24-S struck the Director of Information Technology and the Director of Enrollment and Student Experience and moved their duties to the two vice presidencies. Bill 29-24-S raised the grade point average floors to 2.75 for executive officers and 2.5 for senators and justices. Bill 26-24-S required removal after a second censure in one academic year. Bill 32-24-S moved committee-head appointments from the president to the speaker. Bill 22-24-S changed the amendment rule from two-thirds of the full Senate to two-thirds of the senators present. The bill sheets record no vote counts, and no referendum on these amendments appears in the archive, though the changes are in the constitution WKU has posted since.

In the April 2025 election students ratified Bill 21-25-S, renaming the diversity committee the Action and Opportunity Committee, with 88 per cent in favour. The Senate adopted a codified rewrite of the Executive and Legislative Bylaws on 24 February 2026.

The legislature

2010-11 through 2016-17

The legislative branch was the Student Senate. It met at 5 p.m. every Tuesday of the academic year unless the Senate voted otherwise. Quorum was a simple majority of members. Legislation took two readings, with the Legislative Research Committee reviewing every item in between and determining its constitutionality. A bill acted within the Senate's own jurisdiction; a resolution recommended university policy or stated the Senate's opinion.

The Senate held thirty-six seats across the period, redrawn twice. Until 2013 they were four senators from each of the four classes, two graduate senators, one Gatton Academy senator and seventeen at-large. Resolution 5-13-S, given its second reading on 26 February 2013 and referred to the spring ballot, cut the class seats to three each, created one senator for each of the six academic colleges, cut at-large seats to fifteen, added a seat for the president or delegated representative of each chartered regional affiliate, and wrote a Regional Affiliates article into the constitution. Resolution 1-13-F, first read 27 August 2013 and given its second reading 10 September 2013, created one Navitas or English as a Second Language international student senator and cut at-large seats to fourteen, citing 498 international students at WKU in fall 2003 against 766 in fall 2012. Bill 1-17-S, passed 30-0 on 14 February 2017, replaced the affiliates with Regional Ambassadors nominated by each regional chancellor and confirmed by two thirds of the Senate and four of the seven executive officers, and added a South Campus liaison chosen by the president.

Attendance ran through the Judicial Council. The bylaws allowed a senator no more than three absences a semester unless the Council excused them; the Secretary of the Senate reported excessive absences to the Council and the Speaker, the member appeared to explain, and the Council decided within seven days. From 17 March 2015 the bylaws required every vote to be by roll call or electronic ballot and the minutes posted within a week. Bill 4-17-S, passed 17-13 on 14 February 2017, required each senator to keep one office hour a week in the SGA offices for the rest of spring 2017.

SGA's posted legislation archive begins in fall 2011. It records 53 numbered items for 2011-12, 52 for 2012-13, 26 for 2013-14, 49 for 2014-15, 38 for 2015-16 and 63 for 2016-17. The 2013-14 figure is short: the posted spring 2014 list stops at Bill 7, while the Herald reported Bill 17-14-S passing on 1 May 2014. Spring 2017 alone reached Bill 29 and Resolution 10.

The Executive Cabinet could veto by majority vote and the Senate could override with two thirds. The posted archive and the resolutions themselves record three vetoes in these years: the 2011 dance marathon bill (B02-11-F), the 2014 smoke-free campus resolution (R11-14-F), and Resolution 1-17-S on including former senators at the 2017 SGA Awards Banquet, which was passed unanimously by the Senate on 21 February 2017 and vetoed by the Executive Council the same day, with the veto not overturned.

The judiciary

1970-71 through 1979-80

The Associated Students kept a judicial body through the whole decade, under two names and at least four sizes. The 1971 Talisman described a seven-member Judicial Committee for 1970-71 that interpreted the constitution, heard appeals of all election disputes, ruled on student traffic violations and considered conduct questions referred by the Dean of Student Affairs. Its photograph caption named eight people and gave David Eyler as chairman. Senior listings in the same volume, and President John Lyne's own essay, called the body the Judicial Council.

Students approved a new Associated Students constitution on 10 December 1970 by 1,397 to 697, with 2,094 voting; the Herald listed strengthening the judicial system among its key issues, and the document went on to the administration and the Board of Regents. Congress passed a further revision 23 to 0 on 2 March 1972, twenty-two votes being needed to pass it, and students ratified it 157 to 16 across 29 and 30 March 1972. The Herald reported that the judicial branch had been revised to clarify judicial processes on campus and that the constitution took effect when officers were installed on 27 April 1972.

Members were appointed, not elected. In April 1972 the Herald described a five-member council made up of people appointed by president Linda Jones or former president John Lyne, chaired by John Knight. That council ruled Kim Weaver ineligible to run for president at 7 p.m. on 5 April 1972; the nine-member Student Affairs Board overruled it the next day, holding Weaver had not had a fair hearing. On 13 April five of the council's eight members disqualified themselves and it declined to hear Bob Hatfield's election appeal. The 1973 Talisman put the council at ten under Don Carter, hearing appeals on parking tickets, dorm campusing and election violations; residence-hall boards sent it about 100 conduct cases in 1971-72.

Marc Levy's charge that treasurer David Payne had spent before the budget was approved was heard on 23 October 1975; the 1976 Talisman reported Levy appeared twice before the council and that it ruled in Payne's favor. Congress opened impeachment proceedings against the council's own chairman, Gerard Faulk, on 16 March 1976, six members moving where the constitution required five. Hearings ran three weeks, and on 6 April 1976 the Herald reported Congress acquitted Faulk. The specific roll call and the length of Faulk's term are not confirmed beyond the headline. Faulk chaired the seven-member judicial branch listed for 1976-77, per that year's SGA organization chart. The Herald reported on 8 October 1976 that the council had dropped a dispute involving the student group Maranatha, and in April 1978 it denied Ed Johnson's appeal against Steve Thornton's election, the judicial council being his last source of appeal after the rules and elections committee had already rejected it. In September 1978 the Herald reported Bob Moore -- ASG president and student regent the year before, per the 1978 Talisman -- ineligible for an ASG post; the account of that issue also describes the chairman's appointment process and a constitutional bar on staff members, though no text either checking pass could read confirms those details or ties Moore's case to the judicial chairmanship specifically.

The governing documents

1966 through 1991

The Associated Students of Western Kentucky University began as a document. A sixteen-member organizational committee, drawn from the class presidents and from the residence halls, departmental clubs, honor and professional groups and the Interfraternity and Panhellenic councils, met from November 1965 on a plan written by Dean of Students Charles Keown. President Kelly Thompson approved the finished constitution on 1 April 1966, telling the committee's chairman, Reed Morgan, and vice chairman, John C. Lovett, that it was "hereby approved." The Herald printed it in full on 7 April. Students ratified it in a four-day vote, 1,812 to 726 on 2,538 ballots counted from the dormitories and from Thursday and Friday polling.

That constitution created four campus-wide executive officers, a Student Congress of twenty-five representative seats, a Judicial Council of seven appointed members, and three constitutional committees: Rules and Elections, Finance, and Student Activities. Seats were distributed by group, three to each of the four class councils, two each to the Interfraternity and Panhellenic councils, the men's and women's residence halls and the campus at large, and one each to the professional and honor groups, the departmental clubs and the general clubs. Terms ran one calendar year from the second week of May. Congress had to have two-thirds of its total membership present to transact business, and could expel a member by three-fourths secret ballot ten days after five members endorsed proceedings. Amendment took two-thirds of the full Congress, then a majority of students voting at a regular election, then the University's approval. The first Congress, elected in May 1966, seated twenty-five people: four officers and twenty-one representatives, with the freshman seats empty.

Congress reopened the text almost at once and never closed it. On 13 February 1969 it voted, 18 to 1 by the Herald's count, to create a revision committee of up to ten members. Four changes went to students in voting that closed on 1 May 1970; a quorum question went on the October 1970 ballot. A wholly new constitution carried a referendum on 10 December 1970, 1,397 to 697, after the Rules and Elections Committee refused to run the vote; it went to the administration and the record says no more about it. Congress approved a further draft on 3 March 1972, and students ratified it 157 to 16 in a two-day referendum on 29 and 30 March, on a turnout below two per cent of 10,133 students. Effective at the installation of officers on 27 April 1972, it renamed the body the Associated Student Government, split the vice presidency into administrative and activities posts, replaced the club, Greek and residence-hall seats with ten representatives-at-large and two from each academic college, and rewrote the judicial article.

Revision continued. Associated Student Government passed constitution amendments in February 1979, and Congress proposed and then approved further constitution changes in March 1983. By 1986 the accumulated text survived as a typed constitution of five executive officers, a Congress of class officers and college, on-campus, off-campus, at-large and freshman representatives, and a Judicial Council of seven with three alternates, bound with a separate sheet of further amendments.

What this is

A year-by-year record of student government at Western Kentucky University, from the ratification of the Associated Students constitution in April 1966 to the present. Every entry names the source it came from.

Corrections

The names here start with the plaque in the SGA Chambers, and the plaque is known to be wrong in places. Where the archive disagrees with it, this site follows the archive and records the change on the corrections page. If you served, or you know that a year here is wrong, the project wants to hear from you: people who held an office can be given the keys to their own year on the contribute page.

What it rests on

Everything here comes from what is publicly available online: the digitised Herald and SGA's own papers on TopSCHOLAR, the Talisman yearbooks, wkuherald.com, the university's news pages and the Wayback Machine. That is a fraction of what happened. Student government has met most weeks for sixty years, and the greater part of it was never written down, or was written down on paper that has never been scanned. A thin year here is a thin record, not a quiet one.

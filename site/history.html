1956 — 2026

The students of Western,
in order to form
a legitimate body.

Student self-government at WKU did not begin with a founding moment. It began with an advisory council that nobody could vote for, collapsed into apathy, got refused recognition by the administration and only became real when 2,538 students walked to a ballot box in April 1966 and settled it.

What follows is the paper trail — every entry carries the archival citation it came from.

Ratification Ballot

Western Kentucky University · April 26–29, 1966

Shall the proposed Constitution of the Associated Students of Western Kentucky University be adopted?

For 1,812
Against 726

Each mark = 100 votes. Passed 71.4% to 28.6%.
President Kelly Thompson approved the constitution on April 1, 1966. James P. “Jim” Haynes was elected first president on May 18.

How it works now

Post-2004 architecture

The 2004 Constitutional Convention replaced a body built from delegated constituencies with three branches drawn from the student body at large. Every enrolled student is a member of SGA and can vote in its elections.

Branch I

Executive Cabinet

  • President — also Student Body President and Student Regent on the Board of Regents
  • Student Body Vice President — sits on the Student Life Foundation
  • Chief Financial Officer — sits on the Budget Executive Committee
  • Chief of Staff and Chief Communications Officer — appointed by the President after the election
  • The Speaker of the Senate sits ex officio

Branch II

Student Senate

  • Senators, a Secretary, a parliamentarian and the Speaker of the Senate
  • Meets weekly; moves bills and resolutions
  • Seven standing committees: Enrollment and Student Experience; Campus Improvements and Sustainability; Legislative Operations; Outreach and Public Relations; Action and Opportunity; Student Mental Health and Well-being; Community Relations
  • Community Relations was added in 2022 — the newest committee

Branch III

Judicial Council

  • A Chief Justice, an Associate Chief Justice and five associate justices
  • Interprets the Constitution and Bylaws
  • Rules on whether legislation is constitutional
  • Holds members accountable on attendance and conduct — and hears censure cases

What I could not find

Open research questions

This is the honest part. These are the holes in the story, and what it would take to close each one.

01

There is no complete list of SGA presidents

Not on WKU's site, not in the archives finding aids and not anywhere else I could reach. We have the first (Jim Haynes, 1966), scattered names across the decades and a solid run from about 2015 forward. Everything between is a gap.

Next step: election-result issues of the Herald run every April. Sixty Aprils gets the full list.
02

When the student regent seat was actually created

A February 1968 Herald piece reports WKU sending lobbyists to Frankfort to back a bill placing a student on the Board of Regents. That is the campaign. I could not confirm the year it became law, or who WKU's first student regent was.

Next step: legislative history of KRS 164.321, plus Board of Regents minutes for the first year a student appears.
03

Minutes are missing for roughly half the years

Nothing digitised for 1966–68, 1970–75, 1980–85, 1991–93, 2000–01, 2004 or 2008 onward. The post-2008 gap is probably a records-transfer problem rather than a loss — SGA has been publishing minutes on its own site for years.

Next step: ask Archives whether the 1970s and 1980s boxes are undigitised or genuinely absent.
04

“Associated Student Congress” vs “Associated Student Government”

Herald coverage from 1968 to 1972 uses both names, sometimes in the same issue. That could be a real structural change, or it could be the Herald's copy desk drifting. It matters for anyone writing about how the body was organised before 1992.

Next step: the 1966 constitution and the 1969 minutes will settle what the chamber was formally called.
05

Where the Senate numbering starts

The Senate seated in fall 2023 was the 23rd. The one seated in April 2026 was the 26th. The count tracks the calendar year exactly, which would put the 1st Senate in 2001 — before the 2004 convention. Plausible but unverified.

Next step: the 2001 and 2002 constitutions on TopSCHOLAR should show when the numbering convention was adopted.
